## Living the Future: How Biotechnologies and Bioinformatics are Quietly Changing Our World

> Exploring the transformative impact of biotechnologies and bioinformatics on health, environment, and society
> Professor Erik Bongcam-Rudloff, Head of Bioinformatics at Department of Animal Sciences, SLU, Sweden

The presentation is structured to build a case for a new paradigm in biology and medicine, moving from a historical analogy to current technologies, future concepts, and a final call for collaboration.

### 1. Introduction: The New Revolution

*   **Summary:** Professor Eric begins by comparing the current era of biotechnology and bioinformatics to the internet revolution of the 1990s. He argues that just as the internet seemed niche before fundamentally changing society, these new biological technologies are poised to reshape our lives at a molecular level. He frames this as a time of immense opportunity, akin to the early days of the internet, where "crazy ideas" can lead to major breakthroughs.
*   **Transition:** This section sets the stage by establishing the magnitude of the change, leading into the specific technologies that are driving this revolution *today*.

### 2. The Present State of Biotechnology: Speed and Scale

*   **Challenges Resolved:** The need for rapid, on-site genetic analysis and early, predictive public health monitoring.
*   **Methods Used:**
    1.  **Portable Sequencing:** Using pocket-sized sequencing machines for in-field analysis. Oxford nanopore portable sequencer.
    2.  **Environmental DNA (eDNA) Surveillance:** Sequencing water from sewage systems to detect pathogens. Clever algorithms are used to analyze this data.
*   **Results:**
    1.  Researchers can identify pathogens on the spot without needing to transport samples to a lab.
    2.  Public health officials can predict epidemics (e.g., new viruses) before they are clinically apparent in hospitals, allowing for better preparation. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the critical need for this capability.
*   **Transition:** The speaker emphasizes that the sheer scale of data (from a virus's 32,000 base pairs to a human's 3 billion) forces us to see the body itself as a "living data system," which is more than just its own genome.

### 3. The "Second Genome": The Microbiome's Role

*   **Challenge Resolved:** The human genome alone is insufficient to explain health and disease. Our bodies are complex ecosystems.
*   **Methods Used:** Studying the "second genome"—the collective genes of all microorganisms living inside us (the microbiome).
*   **Results:** The talk highlights that the microbiome contains vastly more genes (~3 million) than the human genome (~20,000) and produces metabolites that actively modulate our health and even behavior (e.g., hunger). This understanding leads to future applications like:
    *   **Personalized Microbial Fingerprints:** Sequencing an individual's microbiome from birth to monitor health over time.
    *   **AI-Driven Diet Recommendations:** Refrigerators connected to a database of your genome and microbiome could suggest personalized meals, treating "food as medicine."
*   **Transition:** After establishing the importance of our internal ecosystem (genome + microbiome), the talk expands to include the crucial influence of the external world.

### 4. The Exposome: Environmental Impact on Health

*   **Challenge Resolved:** Understanding how external, non-genetic factors contribute to health and disease.
*   **Methods Used:** Studying the "exposome," which is the sum of all environmental exposures an individual encounters throughout life. This includes factors like air and water quality, dietary habits, and psychological stress.
*   **Results:** This section introduces the quote: "Genes load the gun, but the environment pulls the trigger." It emphasizes that a holistic health picture requires integrating data on the genome, the microbiome, AND the exposome.
*   **Transition:** The necessity of integrating these three massive, distinct data sources leads directly to the concept of a single, unified health data ecosystem.

### 5. "One Health": The Integrated Ecosystem

*   **Challenge Resolved:** Data and expertise in human, animal, and environmental health are often siloed, preventing a complete understanding of health.
*   **Methods Used:** The "One Health" concept, which aims to interconnect all this data. **Bioinformatics** is presented as the crucial "language" that can interpret data across these different domains.
*   **Results:** This integrated approach is expected to accelerate biological discoveries, revolutionize drug discovery, and enhance healthcare diagnostics. The speaker also notes that this power necessitates deep **ethical discussions**.
*   **Transition:** Such a fundamental shift in approach and the tools used will naturally create a demand for new skills and professions.

### 6. The Future of Work in Bioinformatics

*   **Challenge Resolved:** The jobs of the future in this field will not have the same titles or descriptions as today's roles.
*   **Methods/Results:** The speaker speculates on future job titles that reflect the new interdisciplinary needs:
    *   **Artificial Intelligence Curator:** An expert who understands the limitations of AI and validates the quality of input data.
    *   **Bio Data Architect:** Designs systems for managing and structuring biological data according to FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable).
    *   **Digital Epidemiologist:** Monitors health trends by analyzing real-time sequence data from the environment (e.g., air, water).
    *   **Microbiome Designer:** Engineers custom gut flora to improve individual health.
*   **Transition:** From the future of work, the talk concludes with a summary of the core message and a direct call to action for the audience.

### 7. Conclusion and Call to Action

*   **Summary:** The central message is that the old model of "one gene, one disease" is obsolete. Understanding complex biological systems requires the combined efforts of many disciplines, groups, and cultures. No single lab can do it alone; only networks can.
*   **Call to Action:** The speaker urges the audience, particularly the younger members, to "embrace the future together." He encourages them to be open, to collaborate, and to get involved in future workshops and training in this rapidly evolving field.

### Q&A Session

*   **Summary:** At the end of the talk, the speaker notes that there are no immediate questions. He humorously interprets this as having either "knocked out" the audience or, hopefully, inspired them.

### Comprehensive Summary

*   **Motivation:** The talk is motivated by the observation that we are in the midst of a biological revolution, powered by biotechnology and bioinformatics, that is as profound as the internet revolution of the 1990s. The goal is to explain the dimensions of this change and inspire the next generation to participate.

*   **Central Argument:** The central argument is that our understanding of health and life itself must evolve from a simplistic, siloed view to a holistic, integrated one. The "One Health" concept is key: to understand an individual's health, we must analyze the interplay between their **genome** (genetic blueprint), their **microbiome** (internal ecosystem), and their **exposome** (environmental exposures). Bioinformatics is the essential tool that makes this integration possible, and collaboration across disciplines is the only way to solve the complex problems ahead.

*   **Conclusion:** The era of the lone scientist making a breakthrough on a single gene is largely over. The future of biological discovery and medicine lies in large-scale, interdisciplinary networks that can collect, integrate, and interpret massive, diverse datasets. This new paradigm requires openness, collaboration, and continuous learning, as the technologies and algorithms are constantly changing.

*   **Potential Next Steps:** The speaker explicitly encourages the audience to get involved by contacting him or his colleagues for **future workshops and training**. The implicit next step for the entire field is to build the collaborative networks and data architecture required to realize the "One Health" vision.


## Understanding Life through Protein Interactions

> Dr. K. Anton Feenstra, Programme director Master Bioinformatics and Systems Biology, VU and UvA Amsterdam, The Netherlands

### Logical Flow and Section-by-Section Summary

The presentation logically progresses from establishing the importance of protein interactions to defining them, discussing various prediction methods, detailing the speaker's machine learning approach, addressing benchmarking challenges, and outlining future directions.

#### 1. Introduction: Why Protein Interactions?

*   **Summary:** The speaker begins by highlighting the fundamental importance of protein interactions for understanding how biological systems function. Using an artist's impression of a synaptic bud, they illustrate that while we can identify all the protein components and their quantities, without knowing their interactions, the system remains a static collection of parts—like an unassembled IKEA closet—rather than a functional machine. This lack of interaction knowledge prevents a true understanding of biological processes.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Moving beyond simply identifying components to understanding their functional relationships.
*   **Methods Used:** Visualizing complex biological machinery (synaptic bud) based on measured protein quantities and locations.
*   **Results:** The visualization, despite its realism in terms of components, fails to convey the dynamic, functional aspect due to the absence of interaction information. This underscores the necessity of studying protein interactions.
*   **Transition:** This sets the stage for defining what "protein interaction" truly means in the context of the talk, as the term can be ambiguous.

#### 2. Defining Protein Interactions

*   **Summary:** The speaker clarifies the often-ambiguous term "protein interaction" as used in scientific literature. They distinguish between:
    1.  **Indirect Interactions:** Mutual dependence (e.g., two proteins depending on a third) or cascades (A influences C, C influences B).
    2.  **Complex Membership:** Proteins found together in a complex (e.g., via co-immunoprecipitation), but not necessarily in direct physical contact.
    3.  **Physical Interaction:** Direct contact between residues of two proteins.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Eliminating ambiguity in the term "protein interaction" to focus on direct, physical contacts.
*   **Methods Used:** Categorization and differentiation of various types of reported "interactions" based on their underlying physical or functional relationship.
*   **Results:** The talk explicitly focuses on "physical interactions" where proteins directly touch, which is crucial for understanding molecular mechanisms.
*   **Transition:** With a clear definition established, the speaker moves to the specific problem of predicting these physical interaction sites.

#### 3. Predicting Protein Interfaces (Sequence-based)

*   **Summary:** The goal is to predict which specific residues within a protein sequence are likely to be part of a physical interaction interface. This is a binary prediction (yes/no) for each residue. The speaker addresses why sequence-based prediction remains vital even with the advent of powerful structural prediction tools like AlphaFold.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Identifying the specific amino acid residues on a protein that form the contact points with another protein.
*   **Methods Used:** Sequence-based prediction, where the input is the protein's amino acid sequence, and the output is a prediction for each residue's involvement in an interface.
*   **Results:** A method to predict interface residues from sequence, which is simpler and avoids the complexities of structural prediction in certain scenarios.
*   **Transition:** The speaker elaborates on the continued relevance of sequence-based methods in the era of AlphaFold.

#### 4. Why Sequence-based Prediction with AlphaFold?

*   **Summary:** Despite AlphaFold's success in predicting protein structures, sequence-based interaction prediction remains important because:
    1.  Not all proteins have known structures, and AlphaFold's accuracy for "hard-to-crystallize" proteins is less certain.
    2.  Predicting interactions with AlphaFold requires prior knowledge of the binding stoichiometry (e.g., A-B, A-A-B), leading to a combinatorial explosion of possibilities if unknown.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Limitations of structure-based prediction (like AlphaFold) when structural data is scarce or binding stoichiometry is unknown.
*   **Methods Used:** Highlighting the advantages of sequence-based prediction, which bypasses these structural and combinatorial complexities.
*   **Results:** Sequence-based methods offer a practical and often faster alternative, especially for large-scale screening or when detailed structural information is unavailable.
*   **Transition:** This leads to a broader overview of the spectrum of computational methods available for predicting protein interactions.

#### 5. Methods for Protein Interaction Prediction

*   **Summary:** The speaker presents a spectrum of computational methods for predicting protein interactions, ranging from fast but indirect to slow but accurate:
    *   **Phylogeny:** Very fast, uses evolutionary conservation, but can indicate indirect relationships.
    *   **Docking:** Structure-based, slow, and often inaccurate unless highly constrained.
    *   **Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations:** Slower than docking but more accurate, capable of quantifying interaction strength.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Choosing an appropriate method based on the desired balance between speed, accuracy, and the type of information sought.
*   **Methods Used:** Overview of phylogenetic analysis, docking algorithms, and molecular dynamics simulations.
*   **Results:** A clear trade-off exists: knowledge-based methods (like phylogeny) are fast but less precise, while first-principles methods (like MD) are slow but highly accurate.
*   **Transition:** A concrete example of molecular dynamics simulations is provided to illustrate its capabilities.

#### 6. Molecular Dynamics Example

*   **Summary:** An example of using Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations to study the interaction between two proteins (MP1 and P14). The simulations calculate the potential of mean force, which estimates the free energy of binding. The speaker compares detailed atomistic force fields (very expensive) with coarse-grained force fields (1000x faster).
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Quantifying binding affinity and understanding the dynamic nature of protein interactions.
*   **Methods Used:** Molecular Dynamics simulations with both atomistic and coarse-grained force fields. [9. VU SB Molecular Dynamics](Biology/VU%20Structural%20Bioinformatics/9.%20VU%20SB%20Molecular%20Dynamics.md) 
*   **Results:** Coarse-grained simulations, despite being significantly faster, can provide comparable insights into binding affinity. A fascinating correlation (0.4) was found between the change in free energy upon mutation (biophysics) and BLOSUM scores (evolution), directly linking physical interactions to evolutionary conservation over millions of years.
*   **Transition:** This detailed example leads to the main focus of the talk: the application of machine learning to protein interaction prediction.

#### 7. Machine Learning for Interaction Prediction

*   **Summary:** The speaker's lab developed a machine learning approach for protein interaction prediction. They used a dataset of ~5,000 proteins with known interaction sites from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). A key advancement was the use of **protein language model embeddings** (e.g., from AlphaFold-related work), which capture complex patterns from raw protein sequences. Their architecture evolved from an ensemble of six models using handcrafted features to a more streamlined ensemble of three models leveraging these powerful embeddings.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Improving the accuracy and efficiency of predicting protein interaction sites from sequence data.
*   **Methods Used:**
    *   Dataset: ~5,000 proteins with known interaction sites from PDB crystal structures.
    *   Input Features: Protein language model embeddings (1,024 values per residue) which learn patterns from unannotated sequences.
    *   Architecture: Ensemble of machine learning models (initially six, refined to three) to combine strengths.
*   **Results:** The use of protein language model embeddings significantly improved prediction performance (Area Under the Curve, AUC, increased from ~0.75 to ~0.8) compared to traditional handcrafted features.
*   **Transition:** The success of machine learning leads to a critical discussion about how to properly evaluate these models and avoid common pitfalls.

#### 8. Benchmarking and Data Leakage

*   **Summary:** The speaker highlights a significant problem in machine learning for bioinformatics: **data leakage**. Many benchmark datasets (like ZK448) have been reused extensively over years, leading to a "meta-training cycle" where the community implicitly learns from the test set. This can artificially inflate reported performance. The speaker's team analyzed this by correlating prediction performance (AUC) with sequence identity to the training data.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Ensuring that reported machine learning performance is genuine and not due to inadvertent data leakage from training to test sets.
*   **Methods Used:**
    *   Correlation analysis: Plotting AUC per protein against its sequence identity to the training data. A positive correlation indicates data leakage.
    *   Comparison with other methods: Benchmarking their model against others, including a method that showed signs of data leakage on a specific dataset.
*   **Results:** Significant data leakage was observed in some methods, where performance was artificially higher for proteins similar to those in the training data. The crucial takeaway is that a test set should **only be used once**; new, unseen data is required for subsequent evaluations.
*   **Transition:** This critical insight into data quality leads to the discussion of future work focused on improving the data itself.

#### 9. Future Work: Better Data for Better Models

*   **Summary:** The speaker emphasizes that "better models need better data." A major limitation in protein interaction studies is the lack of comprehensive metadata (e.g., pH, salt concentration, co-solvents, protein concentrations) in published papers. This metadata is crucial for building more realistic and accurate predictive models. The lab is developing a tool to annotate and extract these experimental details from published literature, creating a "gold standard" dataset to benchmark natural language models for automated data extraction.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** The scarcity of rich, structured metadata for protein interactions in existing scientific literature.
*   **Methods Used:** Developing a tool and a gold standard dataset to facilitate the extraction of experimental metadata from published papers using natural language models.
*   **Results:** The aim is to create richer, more detailed datasets that will enable the development of more robust and context-aware predictive models for protein interactions.
*   **Transition:** The talk concludes with a broader reflection on the human aspect of scientific progress.

#### 10. Conclusion: The Human Element

*   **Summary:** The speaker concludes by emphasizing that despite the breathtaking pace of technological advancements in bioinformatics, the core of science remains human collaboration. The scientific community, with its people and their interactions, is a constant amidst changing methods, data, and challenges.
*   **Key Message:** Science happens between people, and collaboration is essential for progress.

### Q&A Session

*   **Question 1 (LLM for Data Extraction - English Only? & Data Mining?):**
    *   **Part A: Why only English scientific literature for the natural language model, given LLMs' translation capabilities?**
    *   **Answer A:** The majority of published scientific literature is in English, making it the most logical starting point. The team has not yet considered expanding to other languages.
    *   **Part B: Is this project (extracting metadata from papers) essentially a data mining exercise?**
    *   **Answer B:** Yes, in a way, it is a "repair operation." Ideally, such metadata should have been deposited in structured databases upon paper submission (similar to protein structures in the PDB). Since this hasn't happened historically, the project aims to extract valuable data that has been "stored in PDF format" for decades.

### Comprehensive Summary

*   **Motivation:** The talk is motivated by the fundamental challenge of understanding how complex biological systems function. Simply identifying protein components is insufficient; deciphering their physical interactions is crucial for comprehending biological machinery and its role in health and disease. The speaker highlights the need for advanced computational tools to tackle this complexity.

*   **Central Argument:** Understanding life at a molecular level hinges on understanding protein interactions. While structural prediction tools are powerful, sequence-based prediction of interaction interfaces remains vital due to limitations in structural data and the combinatorial explosion of possibilities in complex assemblies. Machine learning, particularly when enhanced with protein language model embeddings, significantly improves these predictions. However, the field faces critical challenges: ensuring rigorous benchmarking to avoid data leakage in widely reused datasets, and the pervasive lack of comprehensive metadata in published literature. Addressing these data-centric issues is paramount for developing truly robust and accurate predictive models.

*   **Conclusion:** Significant progress has been made in computationally predicting protein interactions, with machine learning and protein language models offering powerful new avenues. However, the quality and reliability of these models are directly tied to the quality and richness of the data they are trained on. The scientific community must prioritize rigorous benchmarking practices and undertake "repair operations" to extract crucial metadata from existing literature. Ultimately, scientific advancement in this rapidly evolving field depends on sustained human collaboration and a commitment to improving data infrastructure.

*   **Potential Next Steps:**
    *   Further refine machine learning models for protein interaction prediction, with a strong focus on developing and using truly independent test sets to avoid data leakage.
    *   Continue the development and application of natural language models to automatically extract comprehensive metadata (e.g., experimental conditions like pH, salt, concentration) from scientific literature.
    *   Build and disseminate "gold standard" datasets of annotated metadata to facilitate further research in this area.
    *   Advocate for better data deposition practices in scientific publishing to ensure that valuable metadata is structured and accessible from the outset.
    *   Foster interdisciplinary collaboration to integrate computational predictions with experimental validation, driving a deeper understanding of protein interactions.

## Bioinformatics Goes to Farmers Fields in Africa

> Dr. Andreas Gisel, International Institute of Tropical Agriculture, Nigeria and Italian Research Council, Italy

This symposium talk focuses on the application of bioinformatics and biotechnology to improve cassava, a staple crop, in Africa, addressing issues of food security, nutritional deficiencies, and disease resistance.

### 1. Introduction and Context Setting

**Speaker's Background:** The speaker, a bioinformatician from the Institute of Biomedical Technologies, collaborates with the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) in Africa, specifically working with plants.

**Motivation for Working in Africa:** The speaker highlights the severe food insecurity in many African countries, where a significant portion of the population lives on less than 1.5 Euro per day. Emphasizing the quote "diet is your health," the speaker underscores the critical need for food.

**IITA's Role:** The International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) is introduced as a key partner, with hubs across Africa (Ibadan, Kinshasa, Dar es Salaam, Lukasa) focused on breeding better varieties of crops. The speaker's work is conducted on the IITA campus in Ibadan, Nigeria.

**Importance of Cassava:** Cassava (also known as tapioca or manioc) is presented as a major food crop for over 800 million people globally, predominantly in Africa. Key characteristics include:
*   Highest food energy production per land area (almost double that of rice or wheat).
*   Drought and heat resistant, making it resilient to climate change.
*   Yields well in low-quality soil, serving as a crucial "stock" food for many households.
*   The entire plant is utilized: roots for food (flour), stems for propagation, and leaves for food or animal feed.
*   Gluten-free and low glycemic index, making it an interesting food product for specific dietary needs.

### 2. Project 1: Understanding Cassava Root Development

**Challenge Resolved:** The fundamental question of "when and how these storage roots start forming" was largely unknown, hindering efforts to improve yield.

**Methods Used:**
*   **Field Experimentation:** The team physically dug up cassava roots at different developmental stages to observe their formation.
*   **Phenotypic Differentiation:** Roots were categorized into "potential storage roots" and "potential fibrous roots" based on visual characteristics. A simple labeling experiment with rubber bands confirmed that visually identified fibrous roots did not bulk, while storage roots did.
*   **Bioinformatics (RNA-seq):** Sampled storage and fibrous root samples over time for RNA sequencing. Genes specifically expressed in roots (not leaves) were identified. Further analysis separated genes mainly regulated in fibrous roots from those mainly regulated in storage roots.

**Results:**
*   Identified specific genes that are highly expressed in either fibrous or storage roots, allowing for clear molecular differentiation between the two types.
*   This genetic understanding provides a basis for biotechnological approaches to promote earlier, bigger, and more optimally shaped storage roots.

**Lead to Next Section:** This project established the utility of bioinformatics in understanding fundamental cassava biology, paving the way for applying similar approaches to other critical issues like nutritional content.

### 3. Project 2: Vitamin A Fortification in Cassava

**Challenge Resolved:** Vitamin A deficiency is a significant health problem in Africa (e.g., Nigeria, Congo), and cassava, a staple food, is primarily starch with low vitamin A content.

**Methods Used:**
*   **Observation:** Yellowish cassava varieties were identified in farmer fields, suggesting the presence of pro-vitamin A (beta-carotene).
*   **RNA-seq Analysis:** Different cassava varieties with varying beta-carotene concentrations (white to increasingly yellow) were subjected to RNA sequencing.
*   **Metabolic Pathway Analysis:** Investigated the beta-carotene metabolic pathway to identify potential intervention points. Focused on genes regulating beta-carotene synthesis, particularly in roots (not leaves, where it's part of photosynthesis).

**Results:**
*   While *phytoene synthase (PSY)* and *orange gene (OR)* were previously thought to be key regulators, they were not significantly regulated between white and yellow varieties in this study.
*   A downstream *NCD gene* was identified as clearly regulated, suggesting it pulls away beta-carotene for other metabolic processes (e.g., acetic acid and hormone production).
*   **Proposed Intervention:** The team plans to knock out the *NCD gene* to prevent beta-carotene degradation, thereby increasing its accumulation in cassava roots.

**Lead to Next Section:** This project demonstrated the potential to enhance nutritional value through targeted gene manipulation, leading to the next challenge of disease resistance.

### 4. Project 3: Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD) Resistance

**Challenge Resolved:** Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD), caused by the African cassava mosaic virus, is a major threat to cassava crops, leading to significant yield losses.

**Methods Used:**
*   **Breeder Input:** Breeders identified a Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) on chromosome 12 associated with CMD resistance.
*   **RNA-seq Analysis:** Compared gene expression between sick and resistant cassava plants to identify genes differentially regulated in response to the virus (not regulated between two sick or two healthy plants).
*   **Gene Identification:** Reduced the list of differentially expressed genes within the QTL region to identify a candidate gene.
*   **Virus-Induced Gene Silencing (VIGS):** Experiments were conducted in *Nicotiana benthamiana* (a model plant) using a TRV virus to silence the candidate gene, followed by infection with the cassava mosaic virus.

**Results:**
*   Identified *HTH9*, an endotransglycosylase gene, as a key candidate responsible for resistance, likely involved in cell wall permeability and virus movement.
*   VIGS experiments showed that silencing *HTH9* dramatically reduced virus levels in the model plant.
*   **Next Step:** The team is now ready to apply this VIGS approach directly to cassava to confirm the functional role of *HTH9* in resistance.

**Lead to Next Section:** While genetic approaches are powerful, the next section explores a more nuanced aspect of resistance: epigenetics, which can explain phenotypic differences even in genetically similar plants.

### 5. Project 4: Epigenetics in Cassava (CMD Resistance Anomaly)

**Challenge Resolved:** Two cassava genotypes (117 and 693), considered genetically identical duplicates by breeders (based on GBS analysis), exhibited dramatically different phenotypes: 117 was highly susceptible to CMD, while 693 was highly resistant.

**Methods Used:**
*   **DNA Methylation Sequencing and Data Analysis:** Investigated the DNA methylation profiles of both genotypes, both virus-free and infected.

**Results:**
*   The methylation profile of the resistant genotype (693) remained largely consistent whether virus-free or infected.
*   However, the susceptible genotype (117) showed a dramatic change in its methylation profile upon infection, becoming very similar to the resistant genotype (693).
*   **Conclusion:** This suggests that epigenetic modifications (changes in DNA methylation) play a crucial role in the plant's response to viral infection and can explain phenotypic differences even in genetically similar individuals. The susceptible plant, upon infection, epigenetically shifts towards a resistant-like state.

### 6. Conclusion of Research Projects

The speaker concludes by reiterating how bioinformatics provides the foundation for gene editing technologies to develop improved cassava varieties:
*   **Increased Yield:** Through earlier and bigger storage roots.
*   **Enhanced Nutrition:** By producing more pro-vitamin A.
*   **Disease Resistance:** By developing plants resistant to diseases like CMD.

### 7. Q&A Session Summary

**Question 1: Are GMOs allowed in the field in Africa?**
*   **Answer:** Nigeria is in the process of ratifying GMO use, and Kenya has already done so. There is a significant push in Africa to adopt GMOs as a short-term solution to agricultural problems.

**Question 2: Is GMO regulation necessary to prevent Vitamin A fortification from going the way of Golden Rice?**
*   **Answer:** The speaker, whose professor developed Golden Rice, believes that while breeding for increased Vitamin A is possible, it is a very slow process (10+ years) and often leads to the loss of other desirable physical properties (e.g., cooking quality) that are crucial for local consumption patterns. This makes bred varieties less acceptable to farmers, even if nutritionally superior. GMOs/gene editing offer a faster and more precise alternative without losing other traits.

**Question 3: Can epigenetic knowledge be used in breeding programs?**
*   **Answer:** Yes, epigenetic information can be integrated into genetic selection models. While breeders currently focus on genetic information, the decreasing cost and increasing ease of DNA methylation sequencing and analysis make it feasible to include epigenetic data. The challenge lies in convincing breeders to adopt these new models.

**Question 4: Are there concerns about adverse health consequences of GMOs in Africa, similar to Western fears?**
*   **Answer:** The speaker states there is no clear proof of negative health effects from GMOs. While not a personal "fan" of traditional GMOs, the speaker highlights the shift towards gene editing (e.g., CRISPR), which involves precise changes of a few nucleotides rather than introducing large foreign DNA segments. This approach is still debated as to whether it constitutes a "GMO." The long-term goal is to use CRISPR-based mechanisms to modify methylation profiles, potentially converting susceptible plants into resistant ones without altering their fundamental properties.

### Comprehensive Summary

**Motivation:** The central motivation for this research is to combat severe food insecurity and nutritional deficiencies, particularly Vitamin A deficiency, in Africa. This is addressed by focusing on cassava, a critical staple crop, and leveraging advanced biotechnological tools.

**Research Question/Central Argument:** The overarching research question is how bioinformatics and modern biotechnological approaches can be effectively applied to improve key traits in cassava – specifically yield, nutritional content (pro-vitamin A), and disease resistance (Cassava Mosaic Disease) – to benefit African farmers and populations.

**Conclusion:** The talk concludes that bioinformatics is an indispensable tool for understanding the complex biological mechanisms underlying crucial plant traits. By combining bioinformatics with gene editing technologies, it is possible to precisely modify cassava to achieve significant improvements: developing varieties with earlier and higher-yielding storage roots, increased pro-vitamin A content, and enhanced resistance to devastating diseases like CMD. Furthermore, epigenetic mechanisms play a vital, previously underappreciated role in disease resistance, offering new avenues for intervention.

**Potential Next Steps:** The immediate next steps involve applying the identified genetic targets and gene editing techniques (e.g., knocking out the NCD gene for Vitamin A, silencing HTH9 for CMD resistance) directly in cassava. Long-term goals include integrating epigenetic data into breeding programs and exploring CRISPR-based epigenetic editing to induce resistance in susceptible plants without altering their core genetic makeup.

## Cracking the Code: Bioinformatics and the Fight Against Tropical Diseases - The Setaria Genome Case

> Dr. Senanayake Kanchana, University of Colombo, Colombo, Sri Lanka
 
The symposium talk details the use of bioinformatics to establish *Setaria digitata*, a cattle parasite, as a model organism for drug discovery against human lymphatic filariasis.

### 1. Introduction

**Speaker's Affiliation:** The speaker is from the Institute of Biochemistry, Molecular Biology and Biotechnology, University of Colombo, Sri Lanka, a premier institute in molecular biology in the country. The project was initiated by the late Professor Eric, known as the father of molecular biology in Sri Lanka.

### 2. The Problem: Lymphatic Filariasis

**Disease Description:** Lymphatic filariasis is a debilitating tropical disease caused by parasitic nematode worms, primarily *Wuchereria bancrofti* (90% of infections) and *Brugia malayi* (10%).

**Impact:**
*   Affects 863 million people at risk (2020 statistics).
*   Does not typically kill but causes severe, lifelong disabilities, making it the leading cause of disability according to the WHO.
*   While officially eliminated in Sri Lanka, it remains prevalent in many African countries.

**Challenges with Current Drug Strategies:**
*   **Mass Drug Administration (MDA):** The WHO's program uses a cocktail of three drugs, but these do not kill the adult worms residing within the human host.
*   **Drug Resistance:** There is a significant risk of the parasites developing resistance to existing drugs.
*   **Drug Discovery Limitations:** It is extremely difficult to culture human parasitic worms in a laboratory setting for drug testing, and altering them for lab use is not feasible.

### 3. The Solution: *Setaria digitata* as a Model Organism

**Introduction of *Setaria digitata*:** For decades, *Setaria digitata*, a nematode parasite found in cattle, has been used to test anti-filarial drugs.

**Advantages of *Setaria*:**
*   **Accessibility:** Easily obtainable from cattle, solving the problem of sample acquisition.
*   **Cultivability:** Can be cultured in the laboratory, making it suitable for *in vitro* drug testing.
*   **Similarity to Human Parasites:** Morphological and genetic characteristics are very similar to *Wuchereria bancrofti* and *Brugia malayi*.

**Central Hypothesis:** If *Setaria digitata* is genetically similar to human filarial parasites, it can serve as a powerful model organism for discovering and testing new drugs.

**Challenge:** Despite its historical use, a comprehensive genetic model for *Setaria digitata* was lacking.

### 4. Project: *Setaria digitata* Genome Sequencing and Analysis

**Goal:** To generate a high-quality genetic model of *Setaria digitata* to facilitate the identification of novel drug targets.

**Methods Used:**
*   **Genome Assembly:** Utilized both Illumina short reads and Nanopore long reads to construct a high-quality draft genome.
*   **Transcriptome Sequencing:** Employed RNA-seq to analyze gene expression profiles.

**Results (Genome Quality):**
*   A draft genome assembly is publicly available, with the final, refined assembly awaiting publication.
*   **Genome Size:** Approximately 80 megabytes.
*   **Contiguity:** Achieved a high level of contiguity with 159 scaffolds.
*   **Completeness:** BUSCO analysis indicated a 97% completeness score, suggesting a nearly complete genome assembly.

### 5. Validation of *Setaria* as a Model Organism

**5.1. Identification of Existing Drug Target Genes:**
*   **Method:** Searched the newly assembled *Setaria* genome for 18 previously published and confirmed drug target genes known in other filarial nematodes.
*   **Results:** 17 out of the 18 target genes were successfully identified in *Setaria*. Examples include:
    *   **Glutamate-gated chloride channels:** A target for Ivermectin, a WHO-approved drug.
    *   **Calmodulin:** Involved in fertility and growth.
    *   **Transglutaminase:** Important for cuticle development.
    *   **VMR1 and VMR133:** Genes used in diagnostic assays for filariasis.
*   **Significance:** This confirms that *Setaria* possesses the molecular machinery relevant for studying existing drug mechanisms and identifying new targets.

**5.2. Metabolic Pathway Analysis:**
*   **Method:** Investigated the metabolic pathways, specifically the TCA cycle and nutrient metabolism genes.
*   **Results:**
    *   The TCA cycle in *Setaria* was found to be more complete than previously reported in literature, highlighting the importance of genomic data over assumptions.
    *   Compared to free-living nematodes like *C. elegans*, *Setaria* showed low expression of nutrient metabolism genes, indicating its parasitic dependence on the host for metabolites, which is characteristic of filarial worms.

**5.3. *Wolbachia* Status:**
*   **Observation:** *Setaria digitata* does not harbor *Wolbachia* bacteria, which are endosymbionts that aid in the reproduction of some filarial worms.
*   **Results:** Despite the absence of *Wolbachia*, *Setaria* still possesses the necessary reproductive pathways. This makes *Setaria* a versatile model for studying both *Wolbachia*-free and *Wolbachia*-harboring filarial parasites.

### 6. Impact and Conclusion of the Project

**Impact:** The *Setaria digitata* genome provides strong evidence for its utility as a model organism, which can significantly accelerate drug discovery and aid research into filarial diseases.

**Conclusion:** Bioinformatics has successfully transformed a cattle parasite into a powerful genetic model for understanding and combating human filarial diseases. The demonstrated genetic similarity to other human parasites and the availability of a high-quality genomic resource empower productive drug development efforts. ## Science, Infrastructure and the future of computational biology

> Professor Jaap Heringa, UvA Amsterdam, The Netherlands

### Logical Flow and Section Summaries

The talk is structured as a journey through the history, present challenges, and future of bioinformatics, using personal anecdotes and historical examples to illustrate key points.

#### 1. Introduction: The Changing Nature of Science

*   **Summary:** The speaker begins by acknowledging the difficulty of predicting the future but sets the stage by highlighting how science, particularly life sciences, has become increasingly data-driven, interdisciplinary, and collaborative. He introduces the theme of "scaling up" to bigger questions and the growing importance of spatial context in biological data. The speaker contrasts the old, hypothesis-driven "fishing" approach to science with the new, data-intensive "trawling" approach, exemplified by AI, which gathers vast amounts of data first and sorts through it later.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** Sets the context for why computational infrastructure is more critical than ever.
*   **Methods/Examples Used:** Analogy of fishing with a float versus a large trawler. Mention of multi-omics spatial assays.
*   **Transition:** The importance of AI is underscored by the recent Nobel Prize for AlphaFold, leading into a historical example of a large-scale, data-driven project.

#### 2. The Human Genome Project: A Race for Open Data

*   **Summary:** This section recounts the dramatic "race" between the public Human Genome Consortium and Craig Venter's private company, Celera Genomics. The core conflict was about whether the human genome would be a public good or a privately owned, monetized asset. The speaker highlights the political pressure (from Bill Clinton and Tony Blair) and the last-minute computational heroics of mathematician Jim Kent, whose assembly program allowed the public consortium to release their data just days before Celera.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** The immediate threat of the human genome being privatized was averted, establishing a precedent for large-scale collaborative and open science projects.
*   **Methods/Examples Used:** The story of Jim Kent's makeshift computer cluster and rapid software development. The coordinated press conference with Bill Clinton.
*   **Results:** The human genome sequence was placed in the public domain, fueling countless subsequent research projects.
*   **Transition:** The Human Genome Project's reliance on Sanger sequencing provides a natural lead-in to the subsequent explosion in sequencing technologies.

#### 3. The Data Explosion and Its Paradoxes

*   **Summary:** The speaker illustrates the exponential growth in sequencing speed and data output, contrasting the large, slow machines of the past with modern, portable sequencers like the MinION. This leads to a discussion of several paradoxes:
    1.  **Data Growth vs. Evaporation:** While data generation grows exponentially, data is also lost at an alarming rate due to poor storage practices and lack of maintenance.
    2.  **Computational Power vs. Data Volume:** The rate of data generation (doubling every 5-6 months) far outpaces the growth of computational power (Moore's Law, doubling every 1.5 years), creating a fundamental storage and analysis bottleneck.
    3.  **The DNA Storage Solution:** The ultimate irony is that DNA itself is the most efficient known medium for information storage, though writing to it remains a challenge.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** This section doesn't resolve challenges but rather defines the central problems of data deluge, storage, and loss that the rest of the talk addresses.
*   **Methods/Examples Used:** Graphs showing exponential data growth and data loss over time. Comparison of Moore's Law to the rate of DNA sequencing growth. Mention of George Church's experiment writing a book into DNA.
*   **Transition:** The problem of data management leads to the need for structured, high-quality, and well-funded databases.

#### 4. The Importance of Data Curation and FAIR Principles

*   **Summary:** This section champions the cause of data quality and infrastructure. The speaker quotes Amos Bairoch (of UniProt/SWISS-PROT fame) on the decline in funding for essential databases, despite their foundational importance. The talk then introduces the story of patient advocate Steven Keating, who fought to get access to his own medical data, highlighting the societal and personal importance of data accessibility. This culminates in the introduction of the **FAIR** data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) as a framework for managing data effectively, enabling both humans and machines to use it for new purposes.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** The FAIR principles offer a concrete methodology to combat data loss and make data more valuable by enabling its reuse and integration.
*   **Methods/Examples Used:** Amos Bairoch's slides complaining about funding cuts. The story of Steven Keating. The Linked Open Data cloud diagram. An example of simple reasoning across linked databases to answer "does this bug cause malaria?"
*   **Transition:** The idea of making data "AI-ready" (a new interpretation of FAIR) provides a bridge to the future of AI in the field.

#### 5. The Future: Federated Systems and Hybrid AI

*   **Summary:** The speaker outlines the future of computational biology, which involves scaling up to "super-organismal" studies that integrate environment, nutrition, and health. He predicts that the future of AI will be a hybrid model, combining the pattern-recognition power of large language models with the logical, transparent, and explainable nature of traditional symbolic AI. This is necessary to overcome the "hallucinations" and lack of systematic reasoning in current models. The field is termed neuro-symnolic AI, hybrid AI, and neurosymbolic reasoning. It aims to integrate reasoning techniques (logic programming, prolog, knowledge rule systems) with LLM to reduce hallucinations and improve explainability. The talk concludes by highlighting major European initiatives like the European Health Data Space (EHDS) and the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC), which aim to create a federated data infrastructure. This approach avoids centralizing all data, instead connecting distributed resources, which is presented as the only viable solution to the data storage crisis.
*   **Challenges Resolved:** A federated data model supported by initiatives like EOSC offers a path to sustainably manage and analyze massive datasets without requiring impossible amounts of centralized storage. A hybrid AI approach promises more reliable and explainable scientific discovery.
*   **Methods/Examples Used:** Mention of Dutch/European initiatives (Health-RI, ELIXIR, GO FAIR, EOSC, BioSB). A slide comparing the strengths and weaknesses of deep learning vs. symbolic AI.
*   **Results/Conclusion:** The future is not just about bigger trawlers but about building a smarter, interconnected, and sustainable infrastructure to handle the catch.

### Q&A Session Summary

*   **Question 1: What is your opinion on "copycat articles" that reuse the same datasets to find similar results?**
    *   **Answer:** The speaker acknowledges the problem of "too many articles" and incremental publishing driven by academic pressure. However, he offers a potential positive interpretation: such work could be seen as a form of **reproducibility**, especially if it uses different analysis techniques, software, or statistical methods. But if it's just simple copying, it's a problem. He concludes that the scientific community needs to develop "housekeeping rules" to manage this.

*   **Question 2: If you were Global President of Science Funding, how much of your budget would go to keeping what is already there versus generating new knowledge?**
    *   **Answer:** This question targets the core issue of the talk. The speaker describes the "near-death experience in science" where a successful, widely-used database project is denied follow-up funding because it lacks "novelty." He argues strongly that infrastructure is critically important and that there should be a **shift to invest more in maintaining what already exists**. He calls the obsession with novelty a "crazy" culture problem that needs to be addressed.

### Comprehensive Summary

*   **Motivation:** The talk is motivated by the incredible pace of change in bioinformatics and the life sciences. The speaker aims to provide context for the current data-driven landscape and to sound an alarm about the infrastructural crises (data storage, quality, and funding) that threaten to undermine this progress.

*   **Central Argument:** The central argument is that while new data generation technologies and AI are powerful tools for discovery (the "trawler"), the future of computational biology depends less on generating *more* data and more on building a **sustainable, federated, and FAIR infrastructure** to manage, share, and analyze it. The current scientific culture, which prioritizes "novelty" over the maintenance of essential infrastructure, is a fundamental threat to long-term progress.

*   **Conclusion:** The era of centralized data repositories is becoming unsustainable. The future lies in **federated data services**, where data remains distributed but is made interoperable through standards like the FAIR principles, as championed by large-scale initiatives like the European Open Science Cloud. Furthermore, the next generation of AI will need to be a **hybrid** of machine learning and symbolic reasoning to provide the reliability and explainability required for true scientific insight. Finally, a **cultural shift in science funding** is necessary to recognize the value of maintaining and curating existing data resources.

*   **Potential Next Steps:**
    1.  **Implement Federated Data Systems:** Actively build and participate in networks like the EOSC.
    2.  **Adopt FAIR Principles:** Researchers and institutions must prioritize making their data Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable.
    3.  **Develop Hybrid AI:** The research community should focus on integrating symbolic reasoning with deep learning to create more robust and trustworthy AI tools.
    4.  **Advocate for Funding Reform:** The scientific community must collectively lobby funding agencies to create specific, long-term funding streams for the maintenance and curation of critical data infrastructure, separate from funding for novel research projects.

## Symposium Synthesis: Key Themes and Future Directions

This summary synthesizes the core themes from the "Present and Future of Bioinformatics and Systems Biology" symposium, weaving together the insights from all speakers to present a cohesive view of the field's current state and future trajectory.

### 1. The "One Health" Vision and the Infrastructural Reality

A central theme was the paradigm shift towards a holistic, integrated understanding of biology. Professor Bongcam-Rudloff introduced the "One Health" concept, which posits that true understanding requires integrating data from the genome, microbiome, and exposome across human, animal, and environmental domains. He painted a vision of a future where bioinformatics acts as the universal language for this integration. Professor Heringa echoed this by scaling the vision to "super-organismal" studies, but he also provided a critical reality check. He argued that this grand vision is unachievable without a robust and sustainable data infrastructure. He highlighted the paradox of exponential data growth versus "data evaporation" and the unsustainable nature of centralized data storage.

**Conclusion & Future Development:** The field is moving decisively away from siloed, single-gene studies towards an integrated, ecosystem-level approach. However, this ambition is critically dependent on the practical implementation of solutions like the federated data systems (e.g., European Open Science Cloud) and FAIR principles that Professor Heringa championed. The future requires not just dreaming of integrated data, but actively building and, crucially, *funding the maintenance of* the infrastructure to support it.

### 2. The Rise of Grounded and Responsible AI

Artificial Intelligence emerged as a transformative but double-edged sword. Dr. Vasconcelos presented a state-of-the-art solution to one of AI's biggest pitfalls in science: "hallucination." Her team's use of a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) model for precision medicine, which grounds its outputs in a curated, firewalled knowledge base, represents a major step towards safe and reliable clinical AI. Professor Heringa provided the theoretical framework for this, predicting the future lies in "hybrid AI" or "neuro-symbolic reasoning" that combines the pattern-recognition strengths of LLMs with the logic and explainability of symbolic AI. Dr. Feenstra's work on predicting protein interactions using protein language models demonstrated the raw power of these techniques on a specific problem, but his talk also served as a critical warning about the dangers of data leakage in benchmarking, which can create a false sense of progress.

**Conclusion & Future Development:** AI is set to revolutionize bioinformatics, but generic, off-the-shelf models are not fit for scientific or clinical purposes. The path forward is through specialized, "grounded" AI architectures that prioritize accuracy, verifiability, and explainability. Future efforts must focus on building curated knowledge bases for diverse biological domains, developing hybrid AI systems, and establishing rigorous, community-wide standards for benchmarking to ensure that AI-driven discoveries are both real and reliable.

### 3. Applied Bioinformatics: From Code to Crops and Cures

Two talks powerfully illustrated the real-world impact of bioinformatics in addressing urgent global challenges. Dr. Gisel demonstrated the direct application of genomics and transcriptomics to improve cassava in Africa—enhancing yield, boosting nutritional value (pro-vitamin A), and engineering disease resistance. His work, including the discovery of epigenetic factors in resistance, shows bioinformatics as a frontline tool for food security. Similarly, Dr. Kanchana showed how sequencing the genome of a cattle parasite, *Setaria digitata*, has established it as a viable and desperately needed model organism for developing drugs against human lymphatic filariasis. His project provides a foundational resource that will accelerate research into a neglected tropical disease.

**Conclusion & Future Development:** These case studies prove that bioinformatics is not an abstract discipline but a powerful engine for tangible progress in global health and agriculture. They underscore the critical importance of international collaboration (e.g., IITA, EMNET) in overcoming resource limitations and building local capacity. The future lies in expanding these applied approaches: creating more genomic resources for under-studied organisms, translating discoveries into practical tools for breeders and clinicians, and strengthening the global networks that enable this vital work.

### 4. The Data Quality Imperative: Repairing the Foundations of Science

The most urgent cross-cutting theme was the crisis in data quality and infrastructure. Professor Heringa articulated the macro-level problem: a scientific culture obsessed with "novelty" that fails to fund the essential, long-term maintenance of the databases upon which all research depends. He warned that our collective knowledge is at risk of "evaporation." Dr. Feenstra provided a perfect micro-level case study of this crisis. His work on protein interactions is hampered by the lack of structured metadata (e.g., pH, salt concentrations) buried in PDFs, forcing his team to undertake a "repair operation" to mine this critical information. His exposure of data leakage in common benchmark sets further highlights how poor data practices can undermine the entire field.

**Conclusion & Future Development:** The greatest threat to the future of bioinformatics is not a shortage of data or algorithms, but the systemic neglect of data quality, curation, and infrastructure. A fundamental cultural shift is required within the scientific community and its funding bodies. We must begin to value and reward the creation and maintenance of high-quality, FAIR data resources as a primary scientific output. Future work must involve advocating for this funding reform, developing tools for automated metadata extraction, and establishing rigorous "housekeeping rules" for the use and retirement of benchmark datasets.

## Conference Conclusion: A Call for a Sustainable and Collaborative Future

Across all talks, a clear and unified message emerged. The future of bioinformatics and systems biology is massively collaborative, deeply integrated, and increasingly reliant on AI. However, this exciting future is balanced on a fragile foundation. Without a radical shift in our approach to data infrastructure—prioritizing sustainability, maintenance, and quality (FAIR) over the mere pursuit of novelty—we risk our progress collapsing under its own weight. The era of the lone scientist is over, and the path forward requires building not only smarter algorithms but also more robust, open, and sustainable scientific communities and data ecosystems.
