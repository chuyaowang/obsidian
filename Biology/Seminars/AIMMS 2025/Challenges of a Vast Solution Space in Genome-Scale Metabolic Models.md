# Challenges of a Vast Solution Space in Genome-Scale Metabolic Models

**Speaker:** Ursula Kummer
**Affiliation:** BioQuant, Heidelberg University
**Event:** AIMMS Symposium 2025
#seminar 

---

## Executive Summary

Dr. Kummer addresses a fundamental but often underappreciated problem in constraint-based metabolic modeling: the non-uniqueness of solutions generated by genome-scale models (GEMs) under flux balance analysis (FBA). Even when a model is well-constructed and an optimality criterion is imposed, hundreds or thousands of equally optimal flux distributions exist. Selecting any single FBA solution and drawing biological conclusions from it is therefore unreliable. Through case studies with *Enterococcus faecalis*, Dr. Kummer demonstrates that careful manual model construction, multi-layered data integration — particularly quantitative proteomics via SWATH-MS — and rigorous solution space analysis are all required for biologically meaningful predictions.

---

## 1. Background: Constraint-Based Modeling and the FBA Framework

In the absence of complete enzyme kinetic data, dynamic simulation of metabolic networks is generally infeasible. Constraint-based modeling sidesteps this by assuming a steady state in which net metabolite concentrations do not change over time. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

**N · v = 0**

where N is the stoichiometric matrix and v is the vector of metabolic fluxes. This constraint, combined with thermodynamic bounds on flux directions and measured bounds on transport fluxes, defines a feasible region in flux space — a high-dimensional polytope of permissible metabolic states. (See also [4. VU ISB Stoichiometric Models of Metabolism](Biology/VU%20Introduction%20to%20Systems%20Biology/4.%20VU%20ISB%20Stoichiometric%20Models%20of%20Metabolism.md))

Flux balance analysis then applies an optimality criterion — typically maximum growth rate — to select a specific solution from this polytope. Software tools such as COBRApy return one optimal solution. The crucial issue, which Dr. Kummer places at the centre of her talk, is that this solution is **not unique**: many alternative optimal solutions exist, and the one returned is an artifact of numerical details rather than a reflection of actual cellular behaviour.

## 2. Model Organism: *Enterococcus faecalis* and the Rationale for GEM Construction

*Enterococcus faecalis* is a lactic acid bacterium used in food production (cheese, fermented vegetables) and relevant to understanding acid tolerance in gut microbiota. Kummer's group chose it because it occupies a metabolic niche analogous to other lactic acid bacteria and because detailed experimental data — including proteomics under pH stress — were available.

### Manual Model Reconstruction

Building the GEM followed standard steps:
1. Genome annotation to identify metabolic enzymes
2. Literature mining of biochemical reactions
3. Comparative genomics using related-species models (including work done previously in Amsterdam)
4. Iterative gap-filling to connect input substrates to biomass outputs
5. Transport reaction curation — a particularly labour-intensive but critical step

The group emphasised that transport reactions — how the cell exchanges metabolites with the environment — are often the least well-characterized component of a model and have an outsized influence on predictions.

## 3. Automated Tools: Useful Scaffolds, Not Finished Products

Given the growth of tools for automated GEM reconstruction (Agora, KBase, PubSEED, CarveME, Merlin, and MetaDraft), Kummer's group benchmarked several against auxotrophic data — experimental measurements of which amino acids and carbon sources the organism requires or can grow on — as a validation metric.

**Finding:** None of the automated tools correctly predicted the auxotrophic profile. Some failed to run at all (common for tools maintained by a single PhD student without ongoing support). Others produced models that were biologically plausible in broad strokes but systematically incorrect in detail.

**Conclusion:** Automated tools are useful as starting scaffolds and for quickly generating a first draft, but there is no substitute for manual expert curation validated against experimental data. This message connects to a broader discussion in the field — analogous to the limitations of AI-generated outputs — where tools can generate impressively comprehensive models that are nevertheless wrong in ways that matter for scientific conclusions.

## 4. Adding Constraints: From Medium Composition to Quantitative Proteomics

The solution space can be progressively narrowed by adding experimental constraints. Dr. Kummer walked through this systematically:

### 4.1 Exchange Flux Constraints

Measuring uptake and excretion rates of amino acids and carbohydrates provides the first layer of constraints. Even these basic medium-composition constraints substantially reduce the feasible space and enable prediction of growth vs. no-growth phenotypes (auxotrophic predictions). However, predictions remain non-robust: in a 400-model sample of equally optimal solutions, the predicted fermentation pattern can scatter broadly.

### 4.2 Proteomic Constraints: Why Proteomics Over Transcriptomics?

For complex organisms and even microbes under stress, the correlation between mRNA levels (transcriptomics) and protein abundances (proteomics) is poor. Since enzymes — not transcripts — carry out reactions, proteomic data are inherently better suited for constraining flux bounds.

Kummer's group partnered with collaborators who established **SWATH-MS** (Sequential Window Acquisition of all Theoretical Mass Spectra) as a quantitative proteomics platform. SWATH-MS enables measurement of the full proteome in a single run. By using isotope-labeled **aqua peptides** as internal standards, the method yields absolute protein abundance estimates.

### 4.3 pH-Shift Experimental System

The experimental question was: how does *E. faecalis* adapt its metabolism as pH drops from 7.5 (optimal) to 6.5 (acidified, as occurs during lactic acid fermentation)?

The protocol:
1. Grow cells at steady state (pH 7.5) — collect metabolomics and proteomics
2. Slowly decrease pH to 6.5 — collect samples at multiple transient time points
3. Reach new steady state (pH 6.5) — collect metabolomics and proteomics

### 4.4 Incorporating Proteomics into the GEM

**Step 1 — Validate essential protein coverage.** The GEM predicts which enzymes are essential: removing them eliminates any viable solution. Comparing the list of essential enzymes to detected proteins identifies gaps. Only ~15 essential proteins were undetected (mostly membrane-bound), and these were reactivated with upper bounds reflecting the detection limit. Overall proteome coverage was very good.

**Step 2 — Incorporate fold-change constraints.** Significant changes in protein abundance between pH 7.5 and pH 6.5 are incorporated as constraints on flux bounds, using the crude but pragmatic assumption that protein abundance correlates with Vmax. This narrows the solution space from the pH-stress response data.

**Biological result:** At pH 6.5, the model predicts (and proteomics confirm):
- Greatly increased ATP demand to fuel proton pumping and maintain intracellular pH
- Intensified glycolysis
- Increased uptake of serine and arginine — used for ATP generation, not biosynthesis
- Elevated flux through multiple ATP-generating pathways simultaneously

## 5. A Subtlety: Non-Specific Amino Acid Transporters

An illuminating case study comes from the glutamine synthase (GS) knockout mutant. The model initially predicted that the mutant would still grow on minimal medium without glutamine (because it can convert glutamate to glutamine via an alternative route). Experiments showed the mutant cannot grow. The explanation involves kinetics — the alternative route is kinetically too slow — which stoichiometry-based FBA cannot capture.

But there is a second, subtler issue. *E. faecalis* lacks a dedicated glutamine transporter; it relies on broad-specificity amino acid transporters shared with other amino acids. To import enough glutamine, the mutant must import more total amino acid via these transporters. This means it co-imports other amino acids in excess of its needs. These excess amino acids must either be excreted (costing ATP) or catabolised (also costing energy).

If the GEM does not explicitly represent this transporter promiscuity, it cannot reproduce the mutant's elevated energy cost and atypical amino acid consumption patterns. Adding the mechanistic detail of co-import resolved the discrepancy. **Lesson:** Transporter biology and its energetic consequences matter enormously for accurate prediction under stress.

## 6. Analysing the Solution Space: Three Approaches

### 6.1 Flux Variability Analysis (FVA)

FVA computes the permissible range `[min, max]` for each reaction flux while maintaining the optimal objective. Wide ranges indicate under-constrained reactions; narrow ranges indicate reactions that are tightly determined by the available constraints.

**Limitation:** It is hard to interpret FVA ranges phenotypically. Knowing that LDH flux can vary between 0 and 20 mmol/gDW/h does not directly tell you what fermentation pattern the organism will adopt.

### 6.2 CoPE-FBA (Combination of Pathway Enumeration with FBA)

CoPE-FBA analyses the solution space as a combination of metabolic subnetworks (elementary flux modes or extreme pathways). This reveals the underlying structural components of the solution space rather than just intervals.

### 6.3 Perturbation Sampling

The most informative approach demonstrated by Kummer's group:
1. Select a reaction of interest (e.g., lactate dehydrogenase)
2. Fix its flux to values spanning the permissible FVA range
3. For each fixed value, re-run FBA and record the resulting full flux distribution
4. Analyse the ensemble of 400 sampled models to characterise phenotypic variability

This converts abstract mathematical intervals into concrete, phenotypically interpretable metabolic states.

**Results of perturbation sampling across constraint levels:**

| Constraint level | Fermentation pattern prediction | Robustness |
|---|---|---|
| Unconstrained GEM | Scattered (mixed-acid and homolactic) | Very low |
| Medium composition only | Scattered; single FBA predicts wrong phenotype | Low |
| Medium + metabolomics | Concentrated near experimental pattern | Moderate |
| Medium + metabolomics + proteomics | Further concentrated | Good |

The single FBA solution returned by software under minimal constraints predicted homolactic fermentation. The organism is experimentally not homolactic. Adding medium composition constraints caused the single FBA to switch to predicting the opposite — mixed-acid fermentation — while sampling still showed broad scatter. Only metabolomic constraints brought the prediction into reasonable agreement with reality.

## 7. Q&A

**Q: Can AI and more data improve automated reconstruction enough to reduce the need for manual curation?**
More data and better genome annotations will help. But the talk's experience — none of the available tools passed even a simple auxotrophy benchmark — suggests that manual expert curation will remain necessary for the foreseeable future. The analogy to AI-generated text is apt: tools that promise comprehensive outputs may give you a lot that is plausible but wrong; tools that promise less may give you a smaller amount that is more likely correct. The key is knowing which you have and how to validate it.

**Q: Do co-imported amino acids inflate apparent auxotrophic data?**
Yes, this is very likely. Organisms relying on promiscuous transporters to scavenge a required amino acid will inevitably co-import other amino acids, increasing apparent amino acid consumption and energetic demand. This can confound auxotrophic screens and must be accounted for in models.

**Q: Comparison to kinetic models — is the solution space challenge analogous to parameter identifiability?**
Yes, and Kummer explicitly makes this analogy. In kinetic models, parameter identifiability analysis assesses how well parameters can be determined from data. The solution space problem in FBA is structurally analogous: the question is how many metabolic states are consistent with the data, and how tightly the constraints determine the answer. Treating FBA solution space analysis with the same rigor applied to kinetic parameter identifiability is a key recommendation from the talk.

## 8. Comprehensive Summary

### Core Message

Single FBA solutions are unreliable. The solution space of genome-scale metabolic models is vast, and the solution returned by software reflects numerical details, not biological reality. The field sometimes underappreciates this issue, presenting single-solution FBA predictions with more confidence than is warranted.

The path to reliable predictions requires: (1) careful, manually curated model construction; (2) systematic constraint incorporation from multiple data types (metabolomics, proteomics, transport measurements); and (3) rigorous solution space analysis rather than reliance on a single optimal solution.

### Biological Insight from the *E. faecalis* Case Study

*Enterococcus faecalis* responds to acidification by massively increasing its ATP generation — through intensified glycolysis and amino acid catabolism (serine, arginine) — to fuel proton pumping and maintain intracellular pH homeostasis. This response is captured accurately only when quantitative proteomic data are integrated as flux constraints. The glutamine synthase mutant case reveals that transporter promiscuity and its energetic consequences must be explicitly modelled to reproduce experimental data.

### Limitations

- Protein abundance as a Vmax proxy is crude; post-translational modifications and allosteric regulation are not captured
- Steady-state assumption breaks down during transients
- Membrane protein coverage in proteomics remains incomplete
- Kinetic effects (e.g., glutamine synthase inhibition) are invisible to stoichiometric FBA

### Future Directions

- Hybrid kinetic-stoichiometric models incorporating partial kinetic information
- Automated tools for perturbation sampling as a standard output alongside single FBA solutions
- Community-level standards for solution space reporting in GEM publications
- Machine learning over solution ensembles to predict phenotypic distributions under novel conditions
