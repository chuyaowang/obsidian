# AIMMS Symposium 2025 — Overview and Synthesis

**Event:** Amsterdam Institute for Molecular and Life Sciences (AIMMS) Symposium 2025
**Speakers:**
1. Maria Suarez Diez (Wageningen University & Research)
2. Timmy Paez-Watson (TU Delft)
3. Djordje Bajic (TU Delft)
4. Claudia de Buck (Wageningen University)

## Overview

The 2025 AIMMS Symposium brought together nine talks united by a common concern: understanding and predicting the metabolic behaviour of microorganisms, both individually and in community contexts. The programme spanned from fundamental thermodynamic constraints on single-organism metabolism, through methodological challenges in genome-scale modelling, to ecological questions about community coexistence and biotechnological applications in bioprocessing and food production. Four major cross-cutting themes ran through the symposium: (1) the role of redox chemistry and energy conservation in shaping metabolism; (2) the protein economy of the cell and how to model it; (3) the challenge of solution space non-uniqueness in constraint-based models and the related question of what "typical" metabolic behaviour looks like; and (4) the ecology of microbial communities, including how diversity is maintained and how community interactions can be harnessed or predicted.

## Theme 1: Redox Balance and Energy Conservation as Organising Principles of Metabolism

Several talks converged on the insight that redox cofactor balance — not just ATP yield — is a fundamental organising constraint on microbial metabolism, particularly in anaerobes. This theme was most explicitly developed by [de Kok](Biology/Seminars/Decoding%20Nitrogen%20Metabolis%20in%20Marine%20Heterotrophic%20Bacteria.md), but surfaced also in [Suarez Diez](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Dual%20Substrate%20Uptake%20can%20Release%20Metabolic%20Limitations%20Leading%20to%20Improved%20Synergistic%20Yields.md)  and [Paez-Watson](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Constraint%20Based%20Models%20to%20Understand%20and%20Design%20Microbial%20Co-Cultures%20for%20C1%20Utilization.md).

De Kok argued that fermentative organisms, unlike aerobic respirers, cannot decouple anabolism from catabolism via the respiratory chain. Because NADH and NADPH must be internally balanced, whatever comes out of biosynthesis (net NADH excess) must be compensated by catabolism (producing more reduced products). In *Clostridium pasteurianum*, this explains why the growth-rate-dependent shift in butyrate-to-acetate ratio — puzzling in 30 years of experimental data — deviates from the thermodynamically optimal 1:1 prediction: at high growth rates, anabolic precursor synthesis releases large amounts of NADH, forcing the catabolic network to produce more butyrate to rebalance. An additional mechanism — redirecting ferredoxin electrons to NADPH for biosynthesis rather than releasing them as hydrogen — represents a form of energy conservation entirely independent of ATP that becomes increasingly important at high growth rates.

Suarez Diez discussed the same mechanism, electron bifurcation, in acetogens (talk 1). In *Clostridium autoethanogenum*, organisms running the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway operate at the extreme thermodynamic margin — they generate so little free energy per mol of substrate that conventional substrate-level phosphorylation is insufficient. Electron bifurcation — coupling an endergonic redox reaction to an exergonic one — enables the generation of an ion motive force from reactions that would otherwise fail to produce useful energy. Suarez Diez emphasised that this energetic fragility makes these organisms particularly amenable to constraint-based modelling: their metabolism is so constrained that the solution space is narrow, and models are correspondingly predictive.

Paez-Watson (talk 2) added a third perspective. In polyphosphate-accumulating organisms (PAOs), the core inefficiency is not redox imbalance per se but an ATP-expensive glycogen futile cycle that is required to provide NADH for PHA synthesis under anaerobic conditions. By co-feeding aspartate alongside acetate, the NADH generated by aspartate catabolism directly substitutes for the glycogen-derived NADH, creating a "bi-directional synergy": each substrate compensates for the other's metabolic deficiency. This is an example of redox coupling across substrates rather than across cofactors.

**Synthesis.** All three talks illustrate a unifying principle: in anaerobic and low-oxygen environments, the tight coupling between redox state, energy conservation, and metabolic pathway selection makes metabolism more constrained and more predictable than in aerobic organisms. Electron bifurcation, ferredoxin redirection, and cross-substrate NADH complementation are all variations on the same theme — creative solutions to the fundamental challenge of redox balance without a respiratory chain. The implication for metabolic engineering is that these constraints, rather than being obstacles, are exploitable handles: understanding which cofactor is limiting and providing it from an alternative source is a design principle.

**Open questions.** The precise regulatory logic governing pathway selection under changing redox states remains unclear. De Kok's model predicts the product spectrum but cannot yet predict hydrogen production rates. The mechanistic transition between the acetate/butyrate ratio regimes and its dependence on growth rate warrants dynamic (not just steady-state) modelling. Thermodynamic flux balance approaches incorporating ΔG constraints were discussed but not yet applied.

## Theme 2: The Protein Economy of the Cell — Allocation, Constraints, and Engineering

A cluster of talks ([de Buck](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/E%20Coli%20is%20Proton-Saturated%20Lessons%20Learned%20from%20an%20Improved%20Enzyme%20Constraint%20Based%20Model.md), [Kummer](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Challenges%20of%20a%20Vast%20Solution%20Space%20in%20Genome-Scale%20Metabolic%20Models.md), [van den Bogaard](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Parametrizing%20Protein%20Allocation%20Models%20How%20Evolution%20Shapes%20Enzyme%20Turnover%20Numbers%20to%20Support%20Cellular%20Objectives.md)) addressed protein allocation as a central constraint on metabolism, from different angles: model improvement, constraint incorporation from proteomics, and the challenge of parameterising enzyme efficiencies.

De Buck (talk 4) demonstrated that *E. coli* growing anoxically is "proton-saturated" — using up to 40% of its energy budget simply to export protons and maintain intracellular pH. This was revealed only after building an improved enzyme-constrained model (ecModel) based on a corrected version of the iML1515 GEM, with parameters recalibrated for anoxic conditions. The finding supports a metabolic engineering strategy: engineering pyruvate dehydrogenase to shift NADPH/NADH balance and reverse the transhydrogenase — effectively using an alternative route to generate the proton motive force without consuming ATP. The broader methodological point is that enzyme-constrained models require condition-specific parameterisation; naively switching a model from aerobic to anoxic by setting O₂ uptake to zero produces poor predictions.

Van den Bogaard (talk 7) addressed the parameterisation problem from a different angle. Protein allocation models (PAMs) associate every reaction with a kcat value, but for non-model microorganisms these values are unavailable. Her group developed a framework combining a PAM, a sensitivity analysis (SENSE) to identify rate-limiting enzymes, and a genetic algorithm to optimise kcat values against easily measured exchange fluxes. The key biological insight is that microorganisms have evolved enzyme kinetics in response to environmental constraints, so exchange fluxes encode information about those kinetics. Applied to *E. coli*, the approach recovered kcat distributions close to literature values and improved prediction of growth on alternative carbon sources. The ensemble of optimised models (from multiple genetic algorithm runs) provides a principled representation of parameter uncertainty.

Kummer (talk 6) approached the protein constraint problem from the data integration side. Working with *Enterococcus faecalis*, she showed that the non-uniqueness of FBA solutions renders any single predicted flux distribution unreliable without additional data. Quantitative proteomics (SWATH-MS) provided the strongest constraints, progressively concentrating the solution ensemble onto experimentally observed phenotypes. A recurring motif was that proteomics is not just better than transcriptomics because it is more proximal to enzyme activity — it is qualitatively different because it allows identification of which essential reactions are actually absent, which reactions are up- or downregulated across conditions, and how those changes constrain flux bounds. The glutamine synthase mutant example illustrated a subtlety frequently overlooked: non-specific amino acid transporters mean that supplying a required amino acid inevitably co-imports other amino acids, with non-trivial energetic consequences that must be modelled explicitly.

**Synthesis.** These three talks collectively make the case that the protein budget of the cell is a first-class constraint on metabolism, not a secondary consideration. All three found that standard GEMs without protein constraints gave qualitatively wrong or unreliable predictions under the conditions of interest. Enzyme-constrained models improved predictions in all cases, but their accuracy was heavily dependent on the quality and condition-specificity of the parameters used. There is a natural complementarity between van den Bogaard's approach (estimating kcats computationally from exchange fluxes) and Kummer's approach (bounding fluxes with measured proteomics data): the former fills the parameterisation gap for non-model organisms, while the latter refines predictions when data are available. De Buck's work illustrates the practical payoff — discovering the energetically dominant role of proton export in anoxic *E. coli* was not possible without a well-calibrated ecModel.

**Open questions.** In vitro kcats are often poor proxies for in vivo enzyme efficiency because much of the protein pool is idle in most conditions. The unused protein sector in PAMs captures this statistically but does not explain its mechanistic basis. Post-translational modifications, allostery, and metabolite-level regulation are absent from all current models. Whether the evolutionary relationship between exchange fluxes and kcats demonstrated for *E. coli* transfers to distantly related, non-model organisms remains to be tested in more organisms beyond glucose.

## Theme 3: Solution Space, Optimality, and the Typicality Paradigm

A second methodological thread ran through the symposium, concerning not the parameterisation of models but their interpretation. Two talks addressed this directly — [Kummer](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Challenges%20of%20a%20Vast%20Solution%20Space%20in%20Genome-Scale%20Metabolic%20Models.md) from the GEM/FBA perspective, and [Bajic](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Beyond%20Real%20Species%20Random%20Metabolisms%20as%20a%20New%20Lens%20to%20Microbial%20Community%20Ecology.md) from an ecological modelling perspective — but the issue also surfaced in [van den Bogaard](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Parametrizing%20Protein%20Allocation%20Models%20How%20Evolution%20Shapes%20Enzyme%20Turnover%20Numbers%20to%20Support%20Cellular%20Objectives.md)'s use of ensembles and [de Kok](Biology/Seminars/Decoding%20Nitrogen%20Metabolis%20in%20Marine%20Heterotrophic%20Bacteria.md)'s observation that standard FBA predicts the wrong product spectrum for *C. pasteurianum*.

Kummer's demonstration (talk 6) was perhaps the most alarming for practitioners: for an unconstrained *Enterococcus faecalis* GEM, sampling 400 equally optimal flux distributions produces predictions scattered across almost the entire range of possible fermentation phenotypes. The single solution returned by standard FBA software may predict the exact opposite of the experimental phenotype and is just one of thousands of equally valid solutions. This is not a bug but a fundamental feature of underdetermined linear systems. The solution space only becomes trustworthy when multiple layers of experimental constraints — at minimum metabolomics, ideally plus proteomics — are incorporated. Kummer advocated for making solution space analysis a standard part of GEM-based studies, drawing an analogy to parameter identifiability analysis in kinetic modelling.

Bajic (talk 3) addressed the same underlying problem from the perspective of ecological modelling. The standard approach to community modelling uses GEMs in their optimality mode: each organism is assumed to maximise growth. Bajic argued that real microorganisms are not optimal — they evolved in fluctuating environments where multiple trade-offs shape fitness, not single-objective maximisation. He proposed the "typicality paradigm": instead of asking what an organism does under optimal conditions, ask what a typical viable metabolic network looks like. His approach generates thousands of random minimal metabolic networks by iterative random reaction deletion from a universal metabolic network, mimicking genome streamlining. These random models are suboptimal but viable; they have realistic "leaky" secretion profiles and assemble into communities with cross-feeding interactions resembling real ecosystems. Most strikingly, the model reproduces a counter-intuitive experimental observation — that species from a stable community often fail to coexist in pairwise culture — which Lotka-Volterra models with random interaction coefficients cannot reproduce. The implication is that the topology of metabolic networks imposes non-random constraints on ecological interactions, and that these constraints can only be captured by explicitly metabolic (not abstract) models.

**Synthesis.** Both Kummer and Bajic, from different starting points, arrive at a similar meta-level conclusion: the optimality assumption embedded in standard FBA is either wrong (Bajic) or insufficient to identify a unique, biologically meaningful prediction (Kummer). Together they build a case for a more probabilistic, ensemble-based, or typicality-oriented approach to metabolic modelling. Van den Bogaard's use of ensemble solutions from multiple genetic algorithm runs, and Kummer's perturbation sampling of 400 solution models, are practical implementations of this philosophy. The conceptual gap between the two is that Bajic's random-metabolism approach deliberately generates suboptimal organisms to study community-level emergence, while Kummer and van den Bogaard are primarily interested in predicting the behaviour of real, specific organisms. Nevertheless, the shared insight is that the diversity of possible metabolic states — not just the optimal one — is scientifically informative.

**Open questions.** The mechanism by which metabolic network topology produces emergent competitive exclusion in pairwise culture in Bajic's framework remains to be explained. Whether this emergent property is robust to the specific method of generating random minimal networks is unknown. For practical modelling, the field lacks standardised workflows for reporting solution space uncertainty alongside FBA predictions — an analogy to confidence intervals in statistics. The relationship between Bajic's typicality paradigm and real evolutionary processes (drift, selection, historical contingency) is intellectually fertile but underdeveloped.

## Theme 4: Microbial Community Ecology — Coexistence, Interaction, and Engineering

Four talks addressed microbial communities, ranging from engineered co-cultures for biotechnology ([Suarez Diez](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Dual%20Substrate%20Uptake%20can%20Release%20Metabolic%20Limitations%20Leading%20to%20Improved%20Synergistic%20Yields.md) , [Paez-Watson](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Constraint%20Based%20Models%20to%20Understand%20and%20Design%20Microbial%20Co-Cultures%20for%20C1%20Utilization.md)) to theoretical and experimental studies of naturally occurring diversity maintenance ([Bajic](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Beyond%20Real%20Species%20Random%20Metabolisms%20as%20a%20New%20Lens%20to%20Microbial%20Community%20Ecology.md), [Wortel](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Coexistence%20in%20Microbial%20Communities%20The%20Role%20of%20Metabolites%20and%20Toxins.md)). A fifth talk ([Bendig](Biology/Seminars/AIMMS%202025/Alkaline%20pH-Driven%20Metabolic%20Plasticity%20of%20Lactococcus%20lactis%20FM03.md)) added a single-organism perspective on how environmental conditions shape physiology with implications for community-level starter culture performance.

Suarez Diez (talk 1) took a top-down engineering approach: rationally designing multi-species communities to carry out a complex chemical transformation — converting syngas (CO, CO₂, H₂) to long-chain fatty acids — that no single organism can accomplish. A co-culture of acetogens (*Clostridium autoethanogenum*) and chain-elongating bacteria (*Clostridium kluyveri*) was established and modelled. The modelling revealed that the predicted product profile is highly sensitive to the relative biomass abundances of the two species — a critical and often overlooked challenge in community modelling. An extension to a triculture including *Anaerotignum neopropionicum* for odd-chain fatty acid production was explored; the model confirmed feasibility in a broad region of parameter space, though a stable continuous culture could not yet be achieved experimentally.

Paez-Watson (talk 2) showed that synergistic metabolic interactions can operate not only between organisms but between substrates consumed by a single organism. The discovery that acetate and aspartate co-feeding produces superadditive growth yields in PAOs — because each substrate resolves the other's metabolic bottleneck — has implications for community ecology as well: if organisms in mixed communities produce metabolites that resolve each other's limitations, similar synergies could maintain diversity or enhance community productivity.

Bajic (talk 3) took the most abstract approach: using randomly generated metabolic networks as a proxy for the diversity of real microbes. The striking finding is that cross-feeding interactions are essential for maintaining the "rare biosphere" in simulated communities — when metabolic interactions are eliminated, diversity collapses much faster. Primary consumers of the main carbon source (glucose) dominate, while diverse secondary consumers survive only by feeding on the secretions of others. This mirrors the structure seen in many real microbial ecosystems and provides a mechanistic explanation for diversity maintenance rooted in metabolic network topology.

Wortel (talk 8) connected ecological theory most explicitly to experimental data. She argued that frequency-dependent selection is the primary driver of coexistence, operating either through intraspecific competition (in fluctuating environments, abundant types deplete the resource condition at which they have an advantage) or niche creation (cross-protection, as in the antibiotic resistance case). Her use of kinetic metabolic models to compute the feasible trade-off space between growth at low and high substrate concentrations — as an input to community-level population dynamics — represents a novel bridge between intracellular biochemistry and community ecology. The antibiotic resistance case study provided the most rigorous experimental validation in the symposium: a priori predictions of coexistence regions matched experimental data without parameter fitting.

Bendig (talk 9) introduced a single-organism study of *Lactococcus lactis* under alkaline pH. While not about community ecology per se, the talk is relevant in two ways. First, the multi-layer stress response (metabolic rewiring, cell wall thickening, upregulation of proteolytic and flavour-generating enzymes) illustrates how dramatically an organism's physiology can change in response to environmental cues that lie outside its typical range. Second, the demonstration of metabolic memory — alkaline-habituated cells producing threefold more benzaldehyde even when returned to neutral pH — raises the possibility of using environmental conditioning to programme starter culture functionality, a form of deliberate phenotypic engineering without genetic modification.

**Synthesis.** The community ecology talks span a spectrum from theory to application. Bajic operates at the highest level of abstraction (random metabolisms) and makes claims about emergent properties of metabolic networks in general. Wortel sits at the interface of theory and experiment, validating specific mechanistic predictions. Suarez Diez and Paez-Watson are primarily concerned with engineering, using models to guide design rather than to understand natural diversity. All four share a common methodological challenge: connecting the molecular detail of metabolism to population-level outcomes. The different modelling frameworks used — random minimal GEMs, kinetic resource allocation models, constraint-based co-culture models, dynamic FBA — reflect different trade-offs between generality and specificity, mechanistic detail and tractability.

A recurring theme is the importance of cross-feeding and metabolic interdependence, whether it is the syntrophy between acetogens and chain elongators (Suarez Diez), the substrate synergy that reduces glycogen cycling in PAOs (Paez-Watson), the cross-feeding that maintains the rare biosphere in random-metabolism simulations (Bajic), or the niche creation that enables susceptible bacteria to coexist with antibiotic-degrading strains (Wortel). Metabolic interdependence appears to be not merely a secondary consequence of community assembly but a primary driver of diversity and stability.

**Open questions.** Achieving stable tricultures experimentally remains difficult. The conditions under which predicted coexistence equilibria are evolutionarily stable (resistant to invasion by mutants) require further investigation in multiple systems. The relationship between the feasible phenotype space computed from metabolic models (Wortel) and actual evolutionary outcomes in real communities is not yet quantitatively established. How metabolic memory (Bendig) functions at the molecular level — and whether it can be reliably induced and controlled — is an open biotechnological question.

## Concluding Synthesis

The 2025 AIMMS Symposium presented a coherent if multi-faceted portrait of the current state of metabolic systems biology. Three broad messages emerge. First, **constraints matter enormously** — whether they are thermodynamic (proximity to equilibrium in fermentation), redox (internal balance of NADH, NADPH, and ferredoxin), or proteomic (limited enzyme capacity per cell). Ignoring these constraints leads to wrong predictions; incorporating them, whether through enzyme-constrained models, proteomic data integration, or careful redox bookkeeping, dramatically improves predictive power. Second, **the solution space of metabolic models is vast, and single-point predictions are unreliable**; the field is converging on ensemble and probabilistic approaches that honestly represent uncertainty and treat the diversity of possible metabolic states as scientifically informative rather than as an inconvenience to be minimised. Third, **metabolic interdependence is a driver — not a consequence — of microbial community structure**; from syntrophic co-cultures engineered for industrial chemistry to naturally assembled communities maintained by cross-feeding and cross-protection, the ability of microorganisms to resolve each other's metabolic limitations appears to be a fundamental organising principle of the microbial world. Together, these insights push the field toward models that are simultaneously more mechanistically honest, more probabilistic in their outputs, and more attentive to the community context in which microorganisms actually live.
