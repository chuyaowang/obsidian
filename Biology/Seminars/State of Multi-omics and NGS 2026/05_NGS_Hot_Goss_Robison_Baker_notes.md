# NGS Hot Goss: A Review of NGS News from the Bloggers

**Speakers:**  
- Keith Robison, PhD — Automation Visioneer, Ginkgo Bioworks; author of the OmicsOmics blog  
- Shawn Baker, PhD — Genomics Consultant, SanDiegOmics; former early Illumina employee; co-founder of AllSeek (first online sequencing marketplace)  

**Moderator:** Juliana Lemieux, Deputy Editor-in-Chief, GEN  
**Event:** State of Multi-omics and NGS 2026 Summit (GEN / Illumina)  
**Format:** Panel discussion with moderator questions

---

## Background

The next-generation sequencing (NGS) industry has undergone a dramatic maturation since Illumina's dominance was established in the early 2010s. The core technology — sequencing-by-synthesis on a high-density flow cell — remains the dominant short-read platform globally, but a wave of new entrants (Element Biosciences, Ultima Genomics, Pacific Biosciences, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, and now Roche/Sequin) has introduced competitive pressure, new chemistries, and alternative business models.

The broader context for this panel includes: tightening research funding (NIH/NSF budgets under pressure, federal policy changes affecting academic science); continued difficulty for early-stage genomics companies to raise venture capital (most investment flowing to AI companies); the push by sequencing companies into clinical markets as a path to growth beyond saturating research markets; and the emergence of multi-omics as a commercial strategy for companies seeking to expand beyond DNA sequencing.

Key market dynamics the panel addresses:
- **Illumina's market share:** Approximately 77–78% of the NGS market despite the entry of multiple competitors, down from ~83% pre-competition.
- **Long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore, PacBio):** Maturing platforms with growing clinical traction in rare disease genomics and complex genome assembly.
- **Roche Axelios:** A major new entrant using nanopore-adjacent technology (expanding number sequencing), announced at AGBT; first-generation instrument available summer 2026.
- **Element Biosciences (Aviti and Vitari):** Short-read competitor using a different surface chemistry; pivoting strategy from multi-omics surface to high-throughput sequencing.
- **Ultima Genomics:** Ultra-low-cost sequencing focused on large-scale population genomics; still private and not yet profitable.

---

## Section 1: State of the Field — Exciting But Stagnant

Baker opened with a characterization that both panelists endorsed: the NGS field is simultaneously "incredibly exciting and oddly boring." New entrants and technologies abound, but the market numbers have barely moved. Illumina's share dropped from ~83% to ~77–78% — a small decline given the volume of competitive activity. The total sequencing instrument market has been relatively stagnant in absolute dollar terms, though application-level markets (e.g., minimal residual disease/MRD detection) have grown substantially.

Robison agreed that the difficulty of actually eroding Illumina's dominance reflects deep customer inertia, high switching costs, and the challenge that competing instruments face in achieving both cost parity and performance parity simultaneously.

Baker identified two distinct funding headwinds compounding market stagnation: (1) **research funding** is under genuine pressure (uncertain NIH grant outcomes, delayed payments, policy-driven uncertainty); and (2) **VC funding for early-stage genomics companies** remains difficult, with capital concentrating in AI companies and in a small number of well-capitalized winners rather than being distributed across the sector.

---

## Section 2: Multi-omics — Genuine Insight or Stamp-Collecting?

The moderator asked both panelists to evaluate whether the multi-omics push represents genuine biological insight or data accumulation for its own sake. The framing of the question proved useful.

**Robison** used a Darwinian analogy: Victorian naturalists' obsessive specimen collection was derided as stamp-collecting at the time but ultimately produced the pattern-recognition that enabled Darwin's theory of evolution. The question with multi-omics is similar — are we collecting the right data, and are we equipped to see the patterns? He was cautiously optimistic, particularly about **longitudinal serum proteomics**: if large cohorts can be profiled for serum proteins over time, the resulting datasets will likely reveal actionable signals about aging and disease that could not be detected by any single assay. The technology platform is there (Olink, Illumina Protein Prep, and others); the data are being collected; the patterns remain to be found.

**Baker** endorsed the optimism and added a dimension: the proteomics layer is important because "the technology is cool, but the biology is the thing." RNA was important, and proteomics adds the next layer of regulatory complexity. He was candid about the difficulty: proteomics is far more technically and analytically heterogeneous than DNA sequencing. Where DNA sequencing converged to a relatively unified set of assays and analysis tools, proteomics involves many different technologies looking at different aspects of the proteome (post-translational modifications, abundance, localisation, temporal dynamics) with no equivalent dominant platform. "It's going to stay complicated for a really long time." He interpreted this not as a failure but as evidence that the field is at the beginning of its proteomic era, analogous to where sequencing was in the early 2000s.

---

## Section 3: Commercial Strategy — Vertical Integration vs. Focused Platforms

A notable theme at 2026 industry conferences was a split in corporate strategy between Illumina (pursuing vertical integration across all multi-omics modalities) and newer entrants (focusing on one technology and partnering for everything else).

**Illumina's strategy:** Offering a one-stop-shop for multi-omics — single-cell, spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, genomics, bulk RNA-seq, methylation (five-base sequencing), and the TruPath on-flow-cell sample preparation workflow for clinical settings. The vertical integration strategy is natural for the market leader: size enables acquisition and development of adjacent product lines; it is difficult for smaller competitors to replicate this breadth.

Notably, Olink (a major plasma proteomics platform) has been acquired by Thermo Fisher rather than remaining independent or joining Illumina, which limits the sequencing-focused companies' options for proteomics integration. The consolidation of single-cell companies (10x Genomics being the dominant player) has prompted speculation about future M&A — potential linkages between major single-cell companies and sequencing companies — though the panelists noted this remains speculative.

**Element Biosciences (Aviti and now Vitari):** Originally positioned the Aviti as a platform that could be upgraded in software and chemistry to support multi-omics applications ("the chip is the machine" vision, evoking Ion Torrent's early messaging). Baker and Robison noted that Element has pivoted: the Vitari high-throughput sequencer shares chemistry with the Aviti but is a new box that cannot support multi-omic spatial-type assays at launch. They interpreted this as evidence that the multi-omics spatial strategy had not gained sufficient commercial traction, and that Element is refocusing on competing with Illumina's core sequencing throughput instead. Both noted this creates a trust challenge: repeatedly changing the narrative around long-term roadmaps undermines customer confidence.

---

## Section 4: Long-Read Sequencing — Maturing, Less Buzz

**Oxford Nanopore** has entered a quieter, more mature phase: leadership transitions (new CEO), strategic focus on clinical markets, and a technology that is now integrated into routine scientific workflows rather than generating headline announcements. Robison observed that conference talks increasingly focus on biological content rather than which platform was used — a sign that the platform is being taken for granted as infrastructure, which is actually a mark of success.

In the rare disease space, clinicians are progressively accepting long-read sequencing as necessary for a class of cases where short reads fail, though the debate over whether it should be the first-line test or a reflex test after short-read failure continues. At AGBT, long-read sequencing was particularly visible in plant genomics (complex polyploid genomes requiring haplotype resolution) and clinical rare disease genomics.

**PacBio** was not discussed at length but remains a significant competitor in long-read clinical sequencing.

---

## Section 5: Illumina's Clinical Push

Baker confirmed that Illumina has made an explicit strategic pivot toward clinical markets over the past three years or so. By their own reporting, **approximately 60% of their sequencing revenue is now clinical in origin** (broadly defined to include pharmaceutical research oriented toward clinical endpoints). Baker noted the definition is likely expansive, potentially including pharma research sequencing, but the directional shift is real.

The rationale is structural: once the research market is largely captured, growth requires moving into clinical settings where the total addressable market is much larger, the economics are different (reimbursed testing rather than discretionary research budgets), and the application set is driven by clinical utility rather than curiosity.

Robison highlighted the **TruPath on-flow-cell workflow** as an example of Illumina's clinical strategy: it simplifies sample preparation by loading cells directly onto the flow cell, which appeals to clinical labs that want a streamlined "sample-in, answer-out" workflow rather than complex multi-step library preparation. The cold-chain reduction (announced at the NovaSeq X launch, describing the elimination of dry ice requirements) was similarly framed: logistics simplifications that seem unexciting to researchers are highly valuable to clinical labs managing shipping, cold storage, and reagent handling at scale.

Baker recounted that the cold-chain announcement generated one of the most enthusiastic reactions in the room at the NovaSeq X launch — more so than the instrument itself — illustrating how operational friction is the dominant adoption barrier in clinical sequencing.

Robison added: Ultima Genomics has also captured a portion of the liquid biopsy market by supplying sequencing capacity to liquid biopsy companies (the companies running the tests use Ultima instruments for their sequencing), though Illumina remains dominant in that segment.

---

## Section 6: Roche Axelios — The Clinical New Entrant

Roche's entry into sequencing represents a multi-decade effort. The company acquired **Stratos Genomics** (which developed the *Xpandomer* chemistry — a DNA analogue that is physically expanded to improve readability) and **Genia Technologies** (nanopore sequencing) years ago and combined them into the Axelios platform, which began instrument availability in summer 2026.

Key technical characteristics:
- **Reads Xpandomer molecules**, not native DNA — a fundamentally different chemistry from Illumina or nanopore. Error profile and data quality characteristics differ from Illumina, though the Edwin Kuppen group (Netherlands) presented data at AGBT showing essentially indistinguishable performance in cancer applications relative to Illumina.
- **Speed advantage:** Data streaming begins almost immediately (like Oxford Nanopore), enabling very fast turnaround for applications where low data volumes are sufficient (e.g., NICU rapid rare disease diagnosis in hours). The Broad Clinical Labs is launching a rapid newborn sequencing program targeting this market.
- **Flexible economics:** A single reusable chip (~$150, reused up to ~20 times) can support both low-throughput and high-throughput runs, breaking the traditional Illumina trade-off between cost and throughput (which scales nearly linearly from iSeq to NovaSeq). If accurate, this could make Axelios economically attractive across a wide range of sample volumes.

Baker and Robison both emphasized that Axelios is a **first-generation instrument** with a narrow launch collaborator set. Core labs are reluctant to invest in unproven platforms with tight budgets; the instrument needs to prove itself in independent hands before widespread adoption. Robison drew the distinction between hand-picked collaborator results (optimized protocols, close technical support) and real-world performance at diverse institutions.

Roche's clinical infrastructure is its primary strategic advantage: Roche Diagnostics already has global clinical distribution, regulatory relationships, and lab automation expertise. If Axelios can be integrated into Roche's diagnostic portfolio and positioned as a clinical sequencing platform, it has a route to clinical market penetration that pure sequencing companies cannot easily replicate.

## Section 7: Predictions for the Next Year

**Baker:** Market numbers (Illumina market share, total spend) will not change much in one year — the structural factors making change slow are still present. Roche data from independent labs will accumulate and clarify the instrument's real-world performance. Element and Ultima face a structural financial risk: both are private companies that are not profitable and need to raise capital (either VC or IPO) to continue operating. The funding environment for this type of company is difficult, making their sustainability a genuine uncertainty. Multi-omics will continue to develop, but a year is not long enough to expect a step-change.

**Robison:** Roche will appear in more independent hands, and informal feedback ("scuttlebutt") from those early adopters will be the most informative indicator of Axelios' real-world utility. Element and Ultima's financial situations will become clearer — the question is when, not whether, financial pressure becomes a crisis. Illumina's methylation product (five-base sequencing) and the TruPath workflow will likely see updates and more independent data. The field will continue to see progress in spatial multi-omics, with an ongoing parade of new academic spinouts and commercial entrants.

**Lemieux (moderator)** added AI in data handling as a potential major differentiator: the ability of companies to offer intelligent data management and interpretation alongside sequencing instruments may increasingly separate winners from also-rans as raw sequencing costs converge.

---

## Summary

This fast-paced panel discussion offered a grounded and somewhat contrarian take on the NGS field in 2026. The meta-message was that the market is less transformed than the technology announcements suggest: Illumina remains dominant, new entrants have carved niches but not disrupted, and the most important forces shaping adoption are funding constraints, clinical utility requirements, and operational practicalities rather than raw sequencing performance.

The most substantive new development is Roche Axelios, which enters the clinical market with genuine strategic advantages (clinical infrastructure, speed, flexible economics) but as a first-generation instrument that has yet to prove itself in independent hands. The multi-omics trend is real and the panelists were guardedly optimistic about its biological significance, particularly in proteomics, but skeptical of the hype that collecting multi-modal data automatically produces insight. Both panelists converged on the same theme visible across the summit: the biology is what matters, the technology is enabling infrastructure, and the hard work is in the analysis, interpretation, and clinical translation. The dominant bottleneck is no longer sequencing cost — it is understanding what to do with what you've sequenced.
