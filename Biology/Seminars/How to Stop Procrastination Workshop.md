## Cause of procrastination

- **Protection mechanism against negative feelings**  
    Procrastination often functions like an emotional shield: avoiding a task temporarily reduces unpleasant feelings (anxiety, shame, boredom, fear of failure). That short-term relief is reinforcing — your brain learns “avoid task → feel better now,” which increases the chance you’ll avoid next time. Clinically, this is why purely telling yourself to “be disciplined” rarely works: the avoidance is serving an emotional purpose.

- **Chain: Task → Negative feelings → Procrastination**  
    This is a simple cognitive–behavioral model: a task triggers unpleasant thoughts or emotions (e.g., “I’ll never make this work,” or “this will take forever”), those internal events produce discomfort, and avoidance (procrastination behaviors) immediately reduces the discomfort. Over time this cycle strengthens the avoidance habit. The workshop breaks it down because once you can see the link, you can target the steps that maintain the cycle (thoughts, feelings, and the immediate reward of avoidance).

## Exercise to understand procrastination

### Task

- **Prompt:** What concrete activity are you delaying?

- **Example (you):** coding for your project

Why this matters: specifying the task turns vague dread into a concrete target. Vague tasks feel big and amorphous and therefore provoke more negative feelings. The clearer and smaller the task, the less it triggers avoidance.

### Procrastination

- **Prompt:** Describe exactly what you do instead when you avoid the task.

- **Example (you):** watching videos, reading papers, or zoning out

Why this matters: naming the specific avoidant behaviors makes the pattern visible and measurable. That helps you notice when it happens and evaluate whether the behavior truly helps (it usually only helps short-term).

### Excuse

- **Prompt:** What rationales do you use to justify postponing the task?

- **Example (you):** “It’s too late to start today,” “This will take a long time,” “Maybe it’s not that important.”

Why this matters: excuses are automatic cognitive moves that reduce guilt and preserve self-image. Identifying them allows you to test them as beliefs rather than facts — and often to replace them with more accurate statements.

### Feeling

- **Prompt:** What emotions arise when you avoid the task?

- **Example (you):** anxiety, feeling time wasted, stress about progress, overwhelmed by a growing to-do list

Why this matters: procrastination brings a _temporary_ sense of relief at first, but this is usually followed by uncomfortable emotions. Recognizing these feelings helps you see that avoidance is not truly comforting — it often increases distress. Naming the emotions reduces their intensity (a process known as affect labeling) and makes it easier to choose a different response next time.

**Common feelings experienced during procrastination:**

- **Guilt** (“I shouldn’t be doing this instead.”)
- **Shame** (“What’s wrong with me that I can’t just do it?”)
- **Anxiety** (“Time is passing and I’m falling behind.”)
- **Stress or tension** (“My body feels tight just thinking about starting.”)
- **Frustration or irritability** (“Why is this so hard for me? I should already be done!”)
- **Overwhelm** (“There’s so much to do that I don’t even know where to begin.”)
- **Hopelessness or discouragement** (“No matter what I do, I’ll probably mess it up or stay behind anyway.”)
- **Restlessness** (“I keep pacing, scrolling, or switching tasks, but not actually doing the thing.”)
- **Embarrassment when thinking about explaining the delay to others** (“I don’t even know how to tell them why it’s not done—I’ll look ridiculous.”)

### Thought

- **Prompt:** What specific unhelpful thoughts accompany the task?

- **Example (you):** “I must do this to make progress,” “If I don’t do it now I’ll waste time/money.”

Why this matters: these are the cognitive hooks that create urgency or perfectionism. In cognitive-behavioral terms, these are hypotheses you can test (behavioral experiments) and reframe.

#### Common negative thoughts

##### Perfectionism & Fear of Not Being Good Enough

- “If I can’t do it perfectly, I shouldn’t start.”
- “I’m not skilled enough to do this well.”
- “Others would do this better than me.”
- “What if I try and fail? That would prove I’m not capable.”

##### Overwhelm & Task Size Distortion

- “This is too much — I don’t even know where to begin.”
- “I have to finish everything at once.”
- “This will take forever; I don’t have enough time today.”
- “I need a long, uninterrupted block of time to start.”

##### Catastrophic or High-Stakes Thinking

- “If I don’t do well, I will ruin my future.”
- “If this doesn’t work, everything falls apart.”
- “One mistake could mess up the whole project.”

##### Low Self-Belief / Doubt

- “I don’t think I can handle this task.”
- “I always mess things up. Why try?”
- “I’m just not the type of person who can stay consistent.”

##### Minimizing or Devaluing the Task

- “It’s probably not that important anyway.”
- “I don’t really need to do it today.”
- “It won’t matter if it’s late — no one will care.”

##### Avoidance of Discomfort

- “This is going to be unpleasant and frustrating.”
- “I’m tired; I’ll do it when I feel more motivated.”
- “I can’t focus right now — I’ll wait until I’m in the right mood.”

##### Fear of Evaluation or Judgment

- “People will see my work and judge me.”
- “If it’s not great, others will think less of me.”
- “It’s safer not to try than to produce something embarrassing.”

##### Comparisons to Others

- “Everyone else is ahead of me — I’ll never catch up.”
- “People like me just don’t succeed at this level.”

## Positive thinking

### Understand cognitive distortions (common thinking errors)

- **Exaggeration:** turning a setback or difficulty into catastrophe (e.g., “If this fails, I wasted everything”).
- **Black-and-white thinking:** seeing only extremes (perfect completion vs. failure) rather than gradations (partial progress matters).
- **Tunnel vision:** focusing on an immediate stressful detail and ignoring broader context (e.g., ignoring long-term learning, feedback, or other chances to improve).
- **Mental filtering:** Paying attention only to the negative parts of a situation while ignoring anything neutral or positive, making the task seem more unpleasant or risky than it really is.
- **Mind reading:** Assuming you know what others think of your work or performance (usually negative) without evidence, which increases fear of starting.
- **Fortune telling:** Predicting that things will go badly before you begin, treating those predictions as facts instead of possibilities.
- **Catastrophizing:** Imagining the worst-case scenario and treating it as likely, which makes the task feel dangerous or overwhelming.
- **Should statements:** Using rigid rules on yourself (“should,” “must,” “have to”) that create pressure, guilt, and a sense of never doing enough.
- **Labeling:** Turning a temporary difficulty or behavior into a negative statement about your identity, such as calling yourself “lazy” or “incapable.”
- **Personalization:** Taking too much responsibility for setbacks or outcomes, assuming any challenge means you personally failed rather than recognizing normal difficulty or external factors.
- **Emotional reasoning:** Believing that your current feeling reflects objective truth (e.g., “I feel overwhelmed, so the task must be too hard for me”), which discourages action.
- **Discounting the positive:** Minimizing or dismissing your progress and successes, making it harder to recognize growth and stay motivated.
- **Comparative thinking:** Judging your progress only by comparing yourself to others, which often leads to discouragement and avoidance rather than focusing on personal improvement.
- **All-or-nothing time thinking:** Believing a task must be done in one long, perfect block of time, rather than allowing for small, imperfect starts.
- **Disregarding prior evidence:** Ignoring or forgetting past experiences that show you _can_ handle similar tasks or succeed after starting, leading you to assume that “this time will be different” in a negative way and reinforcing avoidance.

Why this matters: these distortions inflate negative emotions and make the task feel unbearable. Identifying the distortion reduces its power and opens the door to calmer, more realistic responses.

### Reframe negative thoughts into more realistic and helpful alternatives

- Try replacing “I have to finish all of it now” with “I can make a small, useful step today.”
- Example (you): “I don’t need to finish everything at once. It’s okay if some ideas fail.”

Why this matters: reframing is not about lying to yourself — it’s about offering a balanced view that reduces emotional intensity and increases the chance you’ll act. Clinically, this supports behavioral activation (doing something despite discomfort) and reduces avoidance.

## Practice having positive thoughts

- **Write the reappraisals down**

  - Keep a short list of realistic, corrective statements (“Small experiments are normal,” “Progress is incremental”).
  - Put them where you’ll see them when you begin (sticky note, first cell in a notebook, file header).

Why this matters: written reminders are external supports for internal practice. They interrupt automatic negative loops and cue action at the moment it’s needed.

- **Associate positive thoughts with a physical anchor**

  - Example anchors: a small object on your desk, a wristband, or a particular mug. Touching or seeing the anchor cues the corrective thought and signals permission to start without perfect conditions.

Why this matters: pairing a cognitive intervention with a sensory cue builds a habit: seeing the object triggers the thought and then the behavior, making it easier to break the avoidance loop.

## Focus on starting, not finishing

### Eat the frog

- **Meaning:** Identify the most important or most uncomfortable task of the day — the one you are most likely to avoid — and do it first. This “frog” is usually the task that would make the biggest positive difference if completed.
- **Why it helps with procrastination:**  
    Procrastination often grows throughout the day as you delay the hard task, which increases dread and mental load. Doing the “frog” first prevents hours of avoidance, reduces anxiety, and gives an early sense of accomplishment that builds momentum.
- **How to apply it without overwhelm:**  
    Eating the frog doesn’t mean finishing the entire big task at once. It means **starting it early in the day**, before easier or distracting tasks steal your energy. Breaking the frog into a small “first bite” (e.g., 10 minutes, one sub-task, or a simple outline) makes it easier to begin without triggering avoidance.
- **Example:**  
    If the “frog” is writing code for your project, your first bite could be opening the project, planning one function, or writing for 10 focused minutes.

### Don't try to find motivation

- **Core idea:** Waiting to “feel motivated” before starting a task is a trap. Motivation is unreliable and often appears _after_ you begin, not before.
- **Why this matters for procrastination:**  
    Procrastination is often fueled by the belief that we need the _right feeling_ to start — energy, inspiration, clarity, or confidence. In reality, these feelings rarely show up on their own. Waiting for them strengthens the avoidance cycle: “I don’t feel ready → I delay → I feel worse → I delay more.”
- **What to do instead:**  
    Focus on taking a small action, even if you don’t feel motivated. This uses the principle of **behavioral activation**: action creates progress, progress creates a sense of capability, and that generates motivation. Once you start, your brain experiences a reward for engaging, which makes continuing easier.
- **Helpful reframe:**  
    “I don’t need to feel motivated to begin. Starting creates motivation.”
- **Example:**  
    Instead of waiting to feel inspired to code, set a 10-minute timer and write anything that moves the project forward. Once you’re in motion, motivation is more likely to follow.

## Practical steps on how to deal with procrastination

1. **Notice the behavior**

    - Develop an early-warning system: a short checklist or log of when you switch tasks. Awareness interrupts automatic avoidance.

2. **Identify the negative thoughts**

    - When you notice avoidance, name the thought out loud or write it down. This converts assumptions into testable statements.

3. **Examine accuracy of the thoughts**

    - Ask: “Is this 100% true? What evidence supports/contradicts it?” Consider milder alternatives and probable outcomes. Use behavioral experiments: try a short, low-cost test and observe results.

4. **Begin with a tiny, clearly defined step**

    - Use implementation intentions (if/then plans): “If it is 10:00, then I will open my project and write for 10 minutes.”

    - Use time-boxing (e.g., Pomodoro: 25/5 or even 10/2) and task decomposition (divide into independent micro-tasks).

5. **Apply self-compassion after setbacks**

    - If you slip, note what happened and what to try next, rather than self-criticism. Self-judgment increases anxiety and fuels more avoidance.

6. **Use environmental design**

    - Reduce easy distractions (turn off autoplay, close irrelevant tabs, put phone in another room). Make the start path frictionless: one click to open your editor, a pre-made script to run tests.

7. **Track progress and reward small wins**

    - Keep a visual log (checked boxes, a brief daily note). Celebrate micro-progress — this reinforces action loops.

8. **Schedule periodic review**

    - Once per week, reflect: what avoidance patterns repeated? What small changes helped? Adjust your plans and cues.

## Short scripts & tools to use immediately

- **If–Then (implementation intention):** “If it is 9:00, then I will open my project and write one comment line.”

- **Behavioral experiment:** “I will code for 10 minutes and record what I accomplished; if I feel okay, I’ll continue.”

- **Mini-Pomodoro with anchor:** Place your anchor object on the keyboard; when you start the 10-minute timer, touch it and read your written reappraisal.

## One-paragraph clinical summary

Procrastination is maintained by a cycle where a task triggers aversive thoughts and feelings, avoidance reduces those feelings immediately, and that short-term relief strengthens the habit. The workshop’s approach — identifying task, avoidance behaviors, excuses, feelings, and thoughts, then practicing cognitive reappraisal and tiny behavioral starts — targets each link in that cycle. Cognitive work reduces the intensity of the aversive reaction; behavioral strategies lower the activation energy to start; environmental design and self-compassion reduce relapse. Together these changes shift your default response from “avoid” to “do a small, testable step,” which over time weakens the avoidance habit and builds sustainable progress.

## Starting Ritual for Breaking Procrastination

This ritual is designed to interrupt the avoidance cycle at the beginning of the day, reduce cognitive load, and activate behavior before negative thoughts and feelings gain momentum. It combines **priority clarity**, **behavioral activation**, and **cognitive reframing**.

### 1. Identify the “Frog” (Daily Priority Clarification)

- **Definition:** The “frog” is the task that is most important for your long-term goals _and_ most likely to trigger avoidance.

- **Clinical rationale:** Procrastination grows when tasks remain ambiguous — the mind fills the gap with threat and discomfort. Selecting one meaningful task reduces overwhelm and provides a clear direction for action, lowering cognitive uncertainty and emotional resistance.

**Daily prompt:**  
_What single task, if progressed today, would meaningfully reduce stress or move me closer to my goals?_  
Write it down in one sentence.

---

### 2. Reduce Activation Energy (Break It into a “First Bite”)

- **Purpose:** Transform the “frog” from an intimidating, high-effort task into a small, approachable starting step.

- **Clinical rationale:** Large or vague tasks trigger anxiety and avoidance. Breaking it into a tiny, concrete step lowers the brain’s threat response and increases the likelihood of initiation.

**Daily prompt:**  
_What is the smallest, 5–10 minute action that would count as “beginning” this task?_  
Examples: open the file, write the outline, run a test, list steps.

---

### 3. Accept That Motivation Is Not Required to Start

- **Principle:** Action often precedes motivation, not the other way around.

- **Clinical rationale:** This follows behavioral activation: engaging in the behavior generates a sense of agency and competence, which then increases motivation. Waiting for motivation reinforces avoidance and strengthens the belief “I can’t start unless I feel ready.”

**Helpful reframe to read aloud:**

> “I don’t need to feel motivated to begin. Starting creates motivation.”

Repeat this each morning before step 4.

---

### 4. Use a Time-Bound Entry (Behavioral Activation)

- **Method:** Commit to a **short, non-threatening start window** (e.g., 10 minutes).

- **Clinical rationale:** A brief, low-commitment time box reduces anticipatory anxiety and bypasses perfectionistic standards. Once engaged, many people naturally continue due to momentum and the brain’s reward response.

**Daily instruction:**  
Set a timer for 10 minutes and begin the “first bite.” You only need to continue if you choose to after time expires.

---

### 5. Reinforce Progress (Reward the Behavior, Not the Outcome)

- **Purpose:** Strengthen the neural pathway that associates starting with a positive experience.

- **Clinical rationale:** Procrastination is reinforced by the relief of avoidance. To reverse this, the brain needs reinforcement attached to _starting and engaging_, not to completing a perfect result.

**After your 10-minute start:**

- Acknowledge the action taken (verbally or in writing).

- Give yourself a small reward — break, stretch, tea, brief pleasant activity.

**Reflection prompt:**  
_How do I feel now compared to before I started?_  
(This builds awareness that anxiety typically decreases after starting.

### Summary of Why This Ritual Works

Procrastination is maintained by a cycle: **task → discomfort → avoidance → temporary relief → future increased anxiety and avoidance**.  
This ritual disrupts the cycle at multiple points:

|Step|Mechanism Countered|Clinical Function|
|---|---|---|
|Identify the frog|Ambiguity & avoidance through lack of clarity|Establishes direction and reduces cognitive resistance|
|First bite|Overwhelm + “all-or-nothing time thinking”|Lowers activation threshold|
|No motivation needed|Emotional reasoning|Promotes action without relying on mood|
|10-minute timer|Anticipatory anxiety & perfectionism|Initiates behavioral activation|
|Reward starting|Relief-reinforcement of avoidance|Builds positive reinforcement for approach behavior|
