# Fireside Chat: AI in Oncology — The View from 2030

**Summit:** State of AI in Precision Oncology 2025 *(Closing Session)*

**Speakers:**
- **Doug Flora, MD** — Executive Medical Director, Yung Family Cancer Center, St. Elizabeth Healthcare; Editor-in-Chief, *AI in Precision Oncology*
- **David Penberthy, MD** — Associate Professor of Radiation Oncology, University of Virginia School of Medicine; President, Association of Cancer Care Centers; former electrical engineer, naval flight surgeon, and Top Gun flight physician

---

> **Books Referenced:**
> - *Chasing My Cure: A Doctor's Race to Turn Hope Into Action* — a memoir by Dr. David Fajgenbaum (referenced in the context of AI-powered drug repurposing and the Every Cure initiative)
> - *Destined for War: Can America and China Escape Thucydides's Trap?* by Graham Allison — referenced in the context of the geopolitical dimensions of the global AI race

## Overview

Dr. David Penberthy delivers the closing address of the summit — a structured, data-driven, and optimistic look at what cancer care might realistically look like by 2030. Drawing on his unusually broad background (engineering, military medicine, radiation oncology, health policy), he synthesizes the day's talks into a big-picture argument: the challenge facing oncology is not just scientific but structural and economic, and AI is the essential technology for navigating a future in which cancer incidence rises by 50%, the healthcare workforce is not keeping up, and the financial sustainability of the system is genuinely at risk. His message, delivered with characteristic pragmatism, is "buckle up, because the best is yet to come."

---

## Part 1: The Magnitude of the Problem

Penberthy opens with global and national cancer statistics to frame the stakes:

**Global cancer burden:**
- ~20 million cancer cases worldwide in 2024
- Projected to increase by more than 50% by 2040, reaching ~30+ million cases

**U.S. cancer burden:**
- ~2 million new cancer cases in 2024
- Projected to exceed 3 million by 2040

**Cancer survivors:**
- 18.1 million cancer survivors in the U.S. today, a number also expected to increase by ~50% in the coming years
- Survivors tilt older and have complex, ongoing healthcare needs — raising the question of who is responsible for their long-term care

He maps cancer incidence and mortality geographically, noting that the middle of America and Appalachia show both high incidence and high mortality, pointing to where resource allocation will have the greatest impact. Breast and lung cancer lead both incidence and mortality statistics, and he notes the encouraging trend in lung cancer mortality — peaking around 1990 and declining since — as evidence that the field's collective work is producing real results.

---

## Part 2: The Structural Crisis

**Healthcare spending:**
In 1960, the U.S. spent 1 in every 20 dollars (~5%) of GDP on healthcare. Today it spends approximately 1 in every 5 dollars (~18–19% of GDP). If cancer incidence rises 50% and reimbursement rates hold flat, the healthcare system faces solvency risk. This is not a hypothetical — it is an arithmetic certainty that policymakers are already grappling with.

**The workforce gap:**
A striking chart shows that since 1970, the number of U.S. physicians has roughly kept pace with population growth — but the administrative workforce in healthcare has grown exponentially. "Every time there's a grand idea about how we can improve healthcare, somebody has to administer it." AI's opportunity here is to reverse this ratio — automating administrative functions rather than adding more humans to manage them.

**The national debt:**
The U.S. carries $38 trillion in debt (as of the talk's data), translating to ~$110,000 per citizen or ~$330,000 per taxpayer. The highest marginal federal tax rate has averaged 57% since 1913. Penberthy notes that tax policy is political and dynamic, but implies that the fiscal pressures on government healthcare spending will shape the incentives for AI adoption.

---

## Part 3: The Information Tsunami

PubMed publishes ~4,000 articles per day, of which ~400 per day are oncology-related. NCCN guidelines have expanded from ~20–30 pages per disease site with ~50 references (early 2000s) to a current average of ~200 pages and ~850 references. The arXiv preprint server in the physical sciences alone receives 1,200 new submissions per day. Penberthy asks the audience directly: how do you stay on top of this?

He cites [Open Evidence](https://www.openevidence.com/) as one example of a tool helping clinicians navigate this information environment — an LLM trained on biomedical literature, partnered with NEJM, NCCN, and JAMA, growing from 0 to 435,000 users in one year (representing approximately 40,000 U.S.-based clinicians using it nearly daily). He describes it as having a co-founder (Zach Ziegler, PhD from Harvard) about his daughter's age and now valued at $6 billion — evidence of how fast the landscape can change.

## Part 4: The Technology Foundations

Penberthy briefly but enthusiastically traces the technology arc:

**The transistor (1947) → modern AI hardware:** The first solid-state transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) won a Nobel Prize nine years later. Today's NVIDIA Blackwell platform has 200 billion transistors in a single chipset; a server rack combines many such chipsets. The smartphone has ~20 billion transistors. Jensen Huang's "Colossus" data center — an old Electrolux vacuum factory in Memphis purchased by xAI, packed with 100,000 GPUs strung together (a configuration previously thought theoretically impossible) — represents the scale of infrastructure now being deployed for AI.

**AlphaGo and AlphaFold:** In 2016, Google DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the world's best Go player using non-intuitive strategies; the game of Go has more possible moves than there are atoms in the universe. Demis Hassabis (now Sir Demis, having been knighted by King Charles after receiving the 2024 Nobel Prize) then created AlphaFold, which solved the protein-folding problem — predicting the 3D structure of all ~200 million known protein sequences — and open-sourced the results for the benefit of humanity. A Netflix documentary, *The Thinking Game*, follows his story. This achievement will unlock drug discovery and mechanism-of-action research at a scale not previously possible.

**Text-to-video generation:** Tools like Sora and Gemini can now generate convincing video from text prompts. Penberthy draws the direct analogy to biology: predicting the next pixel in a video sequence (given all prior context) is structurally similar to predicting the next state of a patient's biology (given their full history). This is the conceptual foundation of digital twins in medicine.

**Wearables and continuous sensing:** Penberthy wears an Oura Ring and Apple Watch personally. He envisions sensor data — continuous physiological monitoring — being used not episodically but as longitudinal health data that AI can analyze to nudge individuals toward better decisions. He notes Japanese companies are already building bathroom-based biosensors that analyze urine for health markers.

## Part 5: AI in Healthcare — The Present and the Horizon

Penberthy characterizes AI in healthcare as already ubiquitous in ways that are often invisible: every Google search is AI; every car navigation system is AI. The question is not whether AI is present but how deliberately it is being deployed.

He surveys the current landscape:
- Doctor-facing tools (ambient documentation, clinical decision support)
- Genetic research tools
- Mental health applications
- Data analytics and scheduling
- Administrative automation

His key frame is the distinction between **artificial intelligence** and **augmented intelligence**: AI incorporating human intelligence into machines, and AI enhancing human performance. He argues the second framing is more accurate and more useful — since the invention of fire and the wheel, humans have used tools to affect the environment. AI is the most powerful such tool ever built.

He summarizes the transformation from current to future state: today's care involves multiple manual steps, indirect data transfer, and cumbersome systems; tomorrow's will feature automated, standardized, error-reduced workflows with richer data for decisions.

---

## Part 6: The Competitive and Geopolitical Context

Penberthy does not shy away from the broader geopolitical dimension of the AI race:

**China:** He shows satellite/aerial views of Shanghai's extraordinary 30-year urban transformation as evidence of a country capable of rapid systemic change. China generates ~9 terawatt-hours of electricity annually (vs. the U.S.'s ~4 terawatt-hours) and is building out new capacity at roughly the equivalent of a new USA every 18 months.

**Thucydides's Trap:** He references Graham Allison's book *Destined for War* and the "Thucydides's Trap" thesis — when a rising power (China) approaches or surpasses the dominant power (the United States), conflict has historically resulted. By purchasing power parity (a CIA-sourced metric), China's economy surpassed the U.S. during the Obama administration. Penberthy's conclusion is not alarmist: "we have to be acknowledging that other economic powers also have a right to exist in the world." But the geopolitical competition has direct implications for AI investment, energy availability, and data infrastructure.

**Energy:** U.S. data centers — Virginia has the largest concentration globally — are at the frontier of compute capability but constrained by energy generation. Some data centers remain unpowered for this reason. This is described as a near-term rate-limiting factor for AI capability expansion.

**Project Stargate:** The current U.S. administration's Project Stargate initiative, partnering with SoftBank, OpenAI (Sam Altman), and Oracle (Larry Ellison), represents a government-level commitment to maintaining AI leadership.

---

## Part 7: Healthcare in 2030 — The Consumer-Centric Model

Citing KPMG's Healthcare 2030 forecast, Penberthy describes a patient population that will expect:
- Digital-first access to care (apps, telehealth, home health)
- Customized provider recommendations and appointment scheduling
- Personalization rather than one-size-fits-all treatment
- Health insurance coverage for telemedicine and home health
- Proactive wellness incentives rather than reactive illness management
- Privacy of data without having to forfeit useful services

He draws a distinction between younger patients (who already expect these things) and older populations still adjusting. His personal advice to his family: "live life above board, because people are going to know everything."

---

## Part 8: Drug Repurposing and AI — The Every Cure Story

One of Penberthy's most evocative examples is the work of Dr. David Fajgenbaum, whose memoir *Chasing My Cure* tells the story of a physician who nearly died of Castleman disease — a rare inflammatory disorder — and used AI-based drug repurposing to identify a treatment that saved his own life, then founded a company called Every Cure to systematize this approach.

The concept: there are ~4,000 FDA-approved drugs and ~18,000+ identified diseases. Matching drugs to unapproved indications through traditional bench research is impossible at scale. AI can cross-reference known drug mechanisms, disease biology, and clinical data to identify promising matches rapidly — effectively unlocking a combinatorial search space no human team could manually explore. Penberthy calls it "unsexy but powerful" and credits it with already saving lives.

---

## Part 9: The 2030 Vision — "What If It All Goes Right?"

Penberthy closes with the theme Flora introduced at the start of the day: what if it all works out?

**Digital twinning for precision medicine:** Rather than testing one drug on a population and measuring average effects, digital twins will allow in-silico simulation of how a specific patient's molecular and clinical profile will respond to a given therapy. "Panomics" — the integration of genomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and all other -omics — will enable a medicine tailored to that individual that may be meaningfully different from what works for the person sitting next to them.

**The Gutenberg moment:** Penberthy places AI in the tradition of civilization's most transformational technologies — the printing press, the automobile (the horse-to-car transition in New York City between 1900 and 1913), the transistor. He uses Astro Teller's (grandson of Manhattan Project physicist Edward Teller) maxim: "it's often easier and faster to make something 10 times better than incrementally better." A Waymo robotaxi across San Francisco — no driver, navigating pedestrians and traffic — is already operating. The hardware in that car is in every Tesla sold today, waiting to be enabled. The same pattern applies to healthcare AI: the hardware and algorithms exist; the integration is coming.

**Education for a new era:** Students using AI tutors learn twice as much in less time than in traditional lecture formats. Future clinicians will need not encyclopedic recall but knowledge curation, probabilistic reasoning, and collaboration with AI systems. The cultivation of empathy and compassion — uniquely human — remains as important as ever.

**The optimist's conclusion:** "What if it all works out? It is going to be the best of times. Radical abundance. We're going to be able to be with our people, living in a healthy state of being." Penberthy ends with a photograph of his daughter at prom and a holiday season wish — a warm, human close to a day filled with data and technology.

## Key Themes and Conclusions

**The cancer burden is increasing faster than the healthcare system can accommodate without AI.** This is not pessimism — it is arithmetic. The response is to leverage AI to multiply the impact of every provider and every healthcare dollar.

**The structural problem is as important as the scientific one.** Administrative bloat, billing complexity, and prior authorization represent enormous drains on clinician time and system resources. AI attacking these problems is not glamorous but is critical.

**AI is already here and will accelerate.** Open Evidence, ambient documentation, digital pathology, and clinical decision support are all operational today. By 2030, digital twins, AI-discovered drug targets, and continuous patient monitoring integrated into clinical care are realistic expectations.

**Geopolitical forces will shape the AI landscape.** China's investment in AI and energy infrastructure, the U.S.'s Project Stargate, and the global competition for compute and talent will determine who builds the healthcare AI of the future and whose values it embeds.

**The "mundane to humane" transformation.** The recurring theme of the day — expressed by both Flora and Penberthy — is that AI's highest purpose in medicine is freeing clinicians from mundane administrative and cognitive work so they can engage more fully in the irreplaceable human dimensions of care: empathy, judgment, and presence.
