# Fireside Chat: Cancer Screening — Hype vs. Reality

**Summit:** State of AI in Precision Oncology 2025

**Speakers:**
- **Doug Flora, MD** — Executive Medical Director, Yung Family Cancer Center, St. Elizabeth Healthcare; Editor-in-Chief, *AI in Precision Oncology*
- **Elizabeth (Betsy) O'Donnell, MD** — Director, Multi-Cancer Early Detection Clinic, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute / Harvard Medical School

---

## Overview

This fireside chat is a wide-ranging conversation on the promise and responsible application of early cancer detection technologies — chiefly multi-cancer early detection (MCED) blood tests and minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring. Both speakers are early advocates of shifting oncology from a reactive, treatment-focused paradigm to a proactive one centered on detection, interception, and prevention. The conversation draws heavily on Betsy O'Donnell's background in plasma cell dyscrasias (multiple myeloma) as a validated proof-of-concept for liquid biopsy approaches, and extends those lessons to solid tumors and broader population screening.

---

## Background: From Reactive to Proactive Medicine

O'Donnell describes her career arc as a deliberate effort to merge two passions: lifestyle medicine and rigorous academic clinical oncology. After spending over two decades at Dana-Farber watching the development of STI-571 (imatinib/Gleevec), monoclonal antibodies, bispecific T-cell engagers, and CAR-T therapies, she became convinced that the prevailing medical paradigm — developing treatments for already-advanced disease — is "a little bit off." The opportunity she identified is applying the same clinical trial rigor to earlier detection and interception, catching diseases at their least genetically complex moment.

Flora frames this shift using a striking shorthand: we currently discover cancers by reading chapter four of the book, when it would be far better to read the first chapter — or even the prologue.

### Concepts

- **Multiple myeloma** is an incurable but treatable blood cancer where cancerous plasma cells accumulate in the bone marrow, overcrowding healthy blood cells and producing abnormal proteins. It commonly causes bone pain, anemia, kidney damage, and high calcium levels (CRAB features).
- **Smouldering multiple myeloma (SMM)** is an asymptomatic, early-stage blood cancer occurring between MGUS and active myeloma, characterized by 10% - 60% bone marrow plasma cells or high M-protein levels without CRAB symptoms.

---

## Plasma Cell Dyscrasias as a Proof of Concept

Multiple myeloma has a well-defined precursor state: monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), which can progress to smoldering myeloma, and then to symptomatic disease. This established disease continuum makes it an ideal model for early detection and interception research. Key elements of O'Donnell's program at Dana-Farber include:

**Screening:** The PROMISE study (led by Irene Ghobrial) has screened ~30,000 individuals who are either African-American or have a family history of myeloma — two populations with elevated risk. Iceland runs a parallel population-based screening initiative. These studies ask who should be screened and with what tools.

**Risk stratification:** Historically, MGUS risk was assessed by the amount of M-protein produced and the bone marrow plasma cell burden. The field is now moving toward AI-assisted analysis of large datasets to generate better prognostic metrics.

**Intervention:** Clinical trials now include lifestyle interventions (fasting, metformin, tirzepatide), microbiome studies, and early introduction of advanced immunotherapies (bispecifics, CAR-T) in high-risk smoldering disease — attempting to cure a disease when it is least genetically complex.

A landmark recent example: daratumumab (a CD38-directed monoclonal antibody) was FDA approved just before this summit for high-risk smoldering myeloma based on the ECILA study, which showed both a progression-free survival and overall survival benefit. O'Donnell describes this as "the turning point" — validating the hypothesis that early intervention matters.

## Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA) and MCED Tests

Flora provides context for the audience: ctDNA are fragments of tumor DNA shed into the bloodstream. Decades of advances in next-generation sequencing and AI-driven pattern recognition have made it possible to detect trace amounts of tumor DNA — and to distinguish the mutational signatures of different cancer types — from a simple blood draw.

O'Donnell explains the framework of MCED tests:
- They take two tubes of blood and scan for abnormal DNA.
- Using AI models trained on catalogued cancer DNA signatures (what lung cancer DNA looks like vs. bladder cancer DNA, etc.), they can report whether a signal is detected and, in many cases, suggest the likely tissue of origin.
- These are **screening tests, not diagnostic tests**. A positive result requires further clinical evaluation.

She acknowledges the legitimate criticisms: false positives, potential for patient anxiety, burden on the healthcare system, and the fact that these are lab-developed tests that bypass the FDA approval pathway required for drugs. Her response is that these criticisms should motivate rigorous clinical trials, not abandonment of the technology.

## Signal vs. Noise: Where Are We Today?

The speakers acknowledge that 70% of incident cancers currently lack any screening test. The benefit of early detection is intuitive: colorectal cancer is 90% curable at stage I but only 12–15% curable at stage IV.

Key data on MCED test performance discussed:
- The **Pathfinder 2 study** (Grail/Galleri test), presented at ESMO, enrolled ~25,000 individuals and reported a positive predictive value (PPV) of ~62%, up from ~40% in Pathfinder 1 — a meaningful improvement.
- O'Donnell reports her clinic's own experience with ~20 positive tests: 78% were true positives leading to a cancer diagnosis. Of the presumed false positives, a retest at a median of ~119 days was negative in every case, consistent with either true false positives or transient signals cleared by the immune system.

Flora and O'Donnell agree that the technology is early — "inning two" — and that the initial misstep by some companies was deploying tests in a broad healthy population (where PPV is necessarily low) rather than in higher-risk populations first.

---

## Minimal Residual Disease (MRD): The Ember in the Ash

MRD refers to the detection of tiny quantities of residual cancer — at sensitivities of 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁶ — after treatment. O'Donnell describes its role in multiple myeloma in detail:

- MRD has become a validated surrogate endpoint, allowing faster drug approvals when progression-free survival takes too long to read out.
- MRD negativity at 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁶ is associated with improved progression-free survival.
- Increasingly, MRD is being used to **guide therapy**: whether to proceed with autologous stem cell transplant (intensification), whether to spare additional treatment in MRD-negative patients (de-escalation), and whether to give patients drug holidays if sustained MRD negativity is achieved.

Flora extends the concept to solid tumors, describing MRD positivity or persistence as "a harbinger of doom" across colorectal, triple-negative breast cancer, melanoma, and bladder cancer in published studies. He uses a memorable analogy: if a CT scan is the helicopter flying above the forest after a fire, looking for wafts of smoke, MRD testing is sifting through the ash for the one red ember that can restart the blaze.

## Looking Forward: Combination Screening and Precision Risk

Both speakers envision a future of **personalized, multi-modal screening** rather than one-size-fits-all guidelines. This might combine:
- Polygenic risk scores
- Metabolomics and microbiome analysis
- Multiple ctDNA modalities (fragmentomics, DNA methylation)
- Immunophenotyping (shifts in T-cell populations seen as cancer evolves)
- Imaging and lifestyle data flowing through integrated EHR platforms like Epic

O'Donnell's group is launching a study in firefighters in Massachusetts and New Hampshire — a high-risk occupational cohort — using low-dose CT, targeted ultrasounds, and MCED blood tests, with the goal of identifying which modalities are most effective and for whom.

Flora notes that there are now ~400 members of Congress who have endorsed the Multi-Cancer Early Detection (MCED) Act (the Nancy Gardner Sewell Act), one of the few bipartisan bills in health care. Once tests receive FDA approval and data matures, reimbursement will be the next critical hurdle to ensure equitable access.

## Key Themes and Conclusions

**The paradigm shift is underway.** The field is moving from reactive treatment of advanced disease toward proactive detection and interception at precursor or early stages. Multiple myeloma already has FDA-approved early intervention data. The evidence base for solid tumor MCED and MRD is accumulating rapidly.

**Rigor is non-negotiable.** The speakers insist that enthusiasm for these technologies must be paired with prospective clinical trials, validated endpoints, and transparent reporting. Marketing claims that outrun the evidence harm the field.

**AI is the enabling technology.** The ability to detect ctDNA in blood, to distinguish cancer-derived DNA from normal DNA, and to identify tissue of origin all depend on machine learning models trained on large, annotated datasets. This is pattern recognition at a scale no human can achieve.

**The "false positive problem" is manageable and overstated.** With appropriate clinical protocols — retesting, follow-up imaging, cancer-type-directed workup — false positive rates can be tracked and managed. The anxiety burden of a false positive is likely lower than the anxiety and clinical burden of a late-stage cancer diagnosis.

**The next horizon is not just detection but interception.** Finding cancer at the earliest signal — when the pathology is least complex, the patient is healthiest, and the immune system may still be capable of responding — opens the door for fixed-duration, potentially curative interventions that could cure 20% more patients and spare years of ongoing therapy.
