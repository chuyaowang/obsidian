# Keynote: Realizing the Promise of Biology's Century

**Summit:** State of AI in Precision Oncology 2025

**Speaker:**
- **Amy Abernethy, MD, PhD** — Co-founder, Highlander Health; former Principal Deputy Commissioner, FDA; former roles at Verily, Flatiron Health, and Duke University

**Moderator:** Doug Flora, MD

---

## Overview

Amy Abernethy delivers a wide-ranging keynote on how AI is transforming oncology from the ground up — across clinical practice, clinical research infrastructure, and drug discovery. Drawing on an unusually comprehensive career spanning bedside oncology, the tech industry, regulatory science at the FDA, and venture capital, she offers a grounded, optimistic vision of what oncology might look like by 2030. The central thesis is that the tools to accelerate access to effective, personalized cancer treatments are now available — what is needed is cross-sector collaboration, trust, and the willingness to redesign how clinical evidence is generated and used.

---

## Speaker Background and Highlander Health

Abernethy's career has been driven by a single question: how do we get treatments that work, safely and effectively, to the people who need them as quickly as possible? This question animated her melanoma clinic at Duke, her move to Flatiron Health to leverage real-world data at scale, her time at the FDA during COVID-19, and her role at Verily. At each step, she identified gaps in the system's ability to bring all the relevant "swim lanes" together.

Highlander Health, co-founded with Brad Hirsch, is her latest attempt to solve this structurally. It operates in two parts:
- **Highlander Health Institute**: a non-profit that grants funding to health systems and academic researchers to test new ideas in clinical research
- **Highlander Health Partners**: a commercial investment arm that backs companies building the infrastructure of modern clinical research and personalized healthcare

Their first acquisition is **Target RWE**, a longitudinal data company in liver disease, gastroenterology, and dermatology — acquired specifically because they believe longitudinal real-world data is the foundational infrastructure for next-generation clinical trials.

## The Role of Optimism and Cross-Sector Collaboration

Abernethy attributes her effectiveness not just to expertise, but to a set of deliberate habits of mind:

**Seeing the possible.** Every improvement she has pursued — whether restructuring data at Duke or helping design COVID-era regulatory guidance at the FDA — began with imagining that the problem could be solved and working backward.

**Valuing all swim lanes.** No single actor in the healthcare ecosystem — academic researchers, community health systems, tech companies, pharma sponsors, regulators — has all the answers. Progress requires building bridges and developing a shared "lingua franca" so these groups can actually talk to each other.

**Mantras for hard work.** During COVID at the FDA, she kept the team grounded with phrases like "we can do hard things and hard things are worth doing." She sees simplicity of message as a leadership tool.

## Lessons from COVID: The Evidence Accelerator

Abernethy's time at the FDA during the pandemic left her with several important lessons that apply directly to oncology's current AI moment.

She helped launch the **COVID-19 Evidence Accelerator** — a recurring multi-stakeholder forum bringing together data holders, researchers, tech companies, and regulators to rapidly make sense of real-world pandemic data (who was getting sick, where ventilators were needed, how interventions were performing).

Key lessons:
1. **The wall problem**: From inside government, it is very difficult to see the possible on the other side of the wall — what tech companies can do, what clinicians are seeing at the bedside. Crucible forums that force cross-sector contact shorten the cycle time dramatically.
2. **Guidance needs examples**: FDA guidance written in a vacuum is abstract and useless. Having pharma sponsors, academics, and data companies bring real examples to the table is what makes regulation workable.
3. **Lingua franca**: All these groups speak different languages, and until they can communicate across those languages quickly, the whole system moves slowly.

## AI Applications Arriving Now and in 2026

Abernethy identifies the most near-term practical AI tools as the ones already developed but not yet fully deployed at scale:

**AI in radiology and digital pathology.** The algorithms exist. The bottleneck is structural — how to route radiology images through AI models embedded in PACS systems, how to digitize pathology workflows. These infrastructure problems are slowly being solved, and the regulatory frameworks already exist.

**Multimodal biomarkers.** Combining imaging data with multi-omics, spatial biology, and clinical data requires both the algorithms and the data plumbing to work together. The "kettle is ready to boil."

**Ambient documentation.** Already showing strong results: note time reduced by 30–50%, biomarker field completeness dramatically improved, physician burnout and after-hours charting substantially reduced. Healthcare systems are willing to pay for this because the value proposition is clear.

**Converting unstructured data to structured research-grade data.** Clinical notes live in PDFs. AI algorithms — LLMs, small language models, and specialized ML models — are now capable of extracting nuanced clinical information from unstructured documents into structured, analyzable formats. This unlocks massive training datasets for downstream algorithms. Abernethy notes the virtuous loop: AI algorithms that curate unstructured clinical notes produce the high-quality structured data that better AI algorithms require.

She is careful to note the challenge of capturing clinical nuance — why the date of a patient's daughter's wedding matters for chemotherapy scheduling, why a three-and-a-half-hour travel distance changes the treatment regimen. Deep learning is getting closer to capturing this, but is not fully there yet.

## The Transparency vs. Invisibility Tension

One of the most important tensions in clinical AI is between two legitimate demands from clinicians:
- **Transparency**: "Show me your reasoning. Tell me which PDL-1 scores and which trial results led to this recommendation."
- **Invisibility**: "Just work in the background and don't add to my click fatigue."

Abernethy argues both are necessary and compatible. Clinicians need confidence that the algorithm is sound and that its inputs are auditable. But they also need to be able to trust it enough to move on — the way they trust a calculator without checking every computation. The key infrastructure piece is ongoing **performance monitoring** — a "cockpit" that tracks algorithm performance in the background and alerts the clinical team when a model is underperforming, hallucinating, or operating outside its validated conditions, so that clinicians can seamlessly revert to standard workflows until the issue is resolved.

Her strategy for skeptical oncologists: get them using AI in their personal lives first (looking up a hamstring injury, checking a drug interaction), then gradually introduce workflow tools. Don't expect everyone to use AI everywhere at once. Let individuals build their own internal tooling and trust over time.

## Oncology in 2030: A Vision

Abernethy sketches a plausible picture of the oncologist's work life four years from now:

**Administrative streamlining.** Billing, reconciliation, and payer interactions will be substantially automated. Prior authorization letters, routing of messages, and documentation will be handled by AI agents in the background.

**Complex multimodal decision support.** The tools available to oncologists will integrate CT scans, digital pathology, multi-omics, and spatial biology in a way that was not previously tractable. AI will help make decisions that account for many more data types simultaneously.

**AI-derived drug targets in the clinic.** The output of AI-based drug discovery will begin appearing in clinical oncology by 2030 — new targets identified computationally, with drugs against them moving through accelerated study designs.

**Patient-facing integration.** Real-time patient data from wearables and apps will flow into the clinical picture in meaningful ways — not creating noise for the oncologist, but surfacing the right signal at the right moment.

**Oncologist wellbeing.** With routine cognitive tasks offloaded to AI, oncologists will have more mental bandwidth for complex clinical reasoning, for spatial biology and multi-omics interpretation, and — Abernethy hopes — for rest.

## Accelerating Clinical Research: The Highlander Health Bet

Highlander Health's explicit goal is to cut clinical research timelines by 50% or more. Abernethy believes the path involves:

**Longitudinal data as infrastructure.** Rather than building registries as afterthoughts, the new model uses tumor registries and longitudinal real-world datasets as the foundational substrate on which clinical trials are layered — "nested clinical trials" already being piloted in Scandinavian countries.

**Modern study designs.** Seamless phase I–III trials where each phase flows directly into the next. 24-hour protocol amendments. Adaptive designs. These are not theoretical — they are being developed now.

**AI for study design, patient finding, and documentation.** Reducing the documentation burden of clinical trials, improving patient matching to eligibility criteria, and enabling smarter study designs.

**Flow-through to clinical decision support.** As clinical trial results are generated, they should automatically flow through to guidelines committees and to patient-facing applications — creating a closed loop between evidence generation and evidence use.

## The Bigger Picture: Oncology as the Proving Ground

Abernethy ends with her "what if it all goes right" answer: oncology will showcase what it looks like to focus on biology, discover new targets, personalize treatment, and make life easier for both the patient and the clinician. By 2035, the lessons learned in oncology — the infrastructure, the mindsets, the tools — will carry over to neurodegenerative diseases, rare diseases, and beyond. What we learn treating glioblastoma will inform how we approach Alzheimer's disease.

Her parting message: trust is the glue that holds it all together. Trust is built slowly, eroded quickly, and requires transparency, tangible value exchange, and consistent behavior from all actors in the system.
